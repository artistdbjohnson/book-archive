# Chapter Six

On the sixteenth day, a little before midday, a boy from the offices came out to my counter. He was maybe twelve, in a clean tunic that was too long for him, and he was out of breath from running down from the upper city. He didn't have anything with him, so he didn't owe anything, and he stood there catching his breath and looking at the two men in the shade of the arch before he looked at me.

"You're to come to the offices," he said. "After you lock up. Today."

"Who wants me?"

"The offices," he said again. That was all he had been told to say, and he had said it, so he turned around and ran back up into the city.

The man with the ink on his finger had watched the whole thing from the shade. He didn't come over. The big man in the brown cloak said something to him, and he nodded.

I worked the rest of the day the same as always. A woman came in with a load of wool on her back, and a man with jars of oil, and two brothers with a cart of bricks. The scarred guard was standing in the bare dust by the post where his onion skins used to be. The count was small again. When the light started to go I locked the box, and I put the key in my pouch, and I walked up into the city.

The streets up that way were wider and cleaner than the ones I lived on, and the people on them walked slower. Most of the shops were closing. A man was sweeping out the front of a perfume seller's, and the street smelled like his shop for a little way, and then it smelled like the city again.

I had been to the offices maybe five times in four years. Once was to sign the contract, and the rest were for the clerk's yearly reckoning, when they went over all the weeks at once. It was a long stone building up past the council hall, with a row of benches out front where people waited, and inside it was one big room with tables, and a man at every table with a board or a tablet or a stack of papyrus in front of him. It smelled like ink and lamp smoke and hot wax. Our clerk was at his table along the side wall. He looked up when I came in, and then he looked back down at his board and didn't look up again.

A doorkeeper took me to a table at the far end. The man there was older than me, with gray in his beard and a good ring on his hand, and I didn't know him. He had a fresh wax board in front of him with nothing on it yet. He didn't ask me to sit, and there wasn't anywhere to sit.

"You hold the northern gate," he said.

"I collect the tolls there."

"Your name."

They had my name. It was on my contract, and the contract was in that building somewhere, in one of the boxes along the back wall. I had put my own mark on it four years before, at a table not twenty steps from where I was standing. He asked me anyway.

"Yohanan," I said.

"Son of?"

"Son of Shimon."

He wrote it down. He wrote it slow and neat, the way a man writes when he means for somebody else to read it later.

"Where do you sleep?"

"The room over the dye works. Below the lower market."

He wrote that down too. Then he looked at what he'd written, and he closed the board.

"That's all," he said.

"What's it for?"

"It's for the board," he said, and he put his hand flat on top of the closed board, the way a man puts his hand on a thing that's his. He didn't say whose board it was going to, and I didn't ask him again. I had asked, and he had answered. That was how it went in that building. Nobody there ever told a man more than he asked, and they didn't always tell him that much.

On my way out I passed the clerk's table. He didn't look up. His charcoal was moving on his board, and I don't think it was writing anything. I wanted to stop and ask him what board it was. I didn't. He had told me to keep my evenings dull, and I didn't think standing at his table in front of all those men was going to help either one of us.

Out front, on the end of the waiting bench, someone had scratched a game board into the stone with the point of a knife, the kind with three squares inside each other and lines across them, and there were two little white pebbles still sitting in it from whoever had been playing last. I stood and looked at it in the last of the light. Then I walked home through the lower market, the way I always went.

Now they had my name twice, on two different boards, and only one of them was for the tolls.

The woman with the basket came on the seventeenth day.

The two men in the arch had gone by then.

They came every day a little before the fourth hour, and lately they'd been leaving around the ninth, when the loads thinned out. I don't think it was luck that she came after they were gone. She came in from the north road on foot, the same as the first time, with her basket on her arm and a cloth over it and the dust up to her ankles. She set it down on the counter and lifted the cloth so I could see the bread, and she counted out the copper for it before I said anything. She didn't look at the arch. She didn't need to. She had watched it from up the road, I think, until it was empty.

I gave her the token. She didn't pick up the basket.

"There's a woman and a boy," she said, low. "They need to go out through your arch before first light. Tomorrow."

I didn't say anything.

"They can't wait until the doors are open and the road is full, and they can't go out by another gate. They've got one bundle, which is cloth she means to sell up north so they can eat, and they haven't got a copper between them." She looked right at me. "That's all of it. I'm not going to tell you more than that."

"The doors don't open until first light," I said.

"I know when your doors open." She picked up her basket. "There's a little door in the big one. I've seen the night watch open it."

She went on into the city. She didn't look back, and she didn't wait to hear what I'd say, and I didn't say anything to her back this time.

There was a little door cut into the left-hand side of the big gate, low, so a man had to duck going through it, and it had its own bar. At night when the big doors were shut, the night watch opened it for somebody on foot if they knew him and he had a reason, a midwife or a man with a sick father out in one of the villages. They wouldn't open it for a stranger. They wouldn't open it for goods, because goods owed me, and I wasn't there at night. That's how it had always been at my gate, and everybody who used that road knew it.

So a woman and a boy with a bundle of cloth weren't going to get through that little door before first light unless somebody they knew was standing there, and the only somebody anyone at that gate knew was me.

I locked up that evening and walked home. I didn't lie down. I sat on the edge of the cot in the dark with my sandals still on, and I listened to the dye works go to sleep below me, and I didn't think about much of anything. I knew I was going. I think I knew it at the counter when she said "before first light," and I sat there for half the night anyway, like sitting there was going to change it.

When it was still full dark, I got up and went.

The streets were empty. The lower market was shut tight, every awning rolled and every door barred. I passed the woodworker's shop with the rag still in the gap of the door. Up near the gate there were a few loads already waiting inside the wall for morning, a donkey man asleep against his sacks and two carts with their drivers rolled up in cloaks underneath them. Nobody looked up.

The big doors were shut and barred. There was one small lamp burning in the niche by the post, and under it, sitting on the bench with his back against the wall, was the scarred guard.

I hadn't known they'd put him on the night watch. His tall partner was asleep at the other end of the bench with his mouth open. The scarred guard wasn't asleep. He watched me come up out of the dark to my counter, and he didn't get up.

"You're early," he said.

"I am."

I unlocked the box and set the tablet in its groove, the same as any morning, and I stood behind my counter in the dark and waited. He watched me do it. He didn't ask me why, and I didn't tell him.

It was cooler than I was used to at that counter. The wall behind me had given up all the day's heat in the night, and the stone was cold against my back when I leaned on it. Down the street the donkey man was snoring, and every so often one of his donkeys shifted and its hobble rope creaked. I stood there and listened to that and watched the mouths of the side streets.

They came out of a side street a little after that. The woman had a scarf over her head and the bundle on her back, tied across her chest, and it was a big one. The boy was holding onto her belt. He was maybe ten, older than the boy in the yard, and he was barefoot. They came straight to my counter, the way she must have been told to, and the woman stopped in front of it and looked at me. She didn't have anything in her hands. She opened her mouth to say something and then didn't say it.

I knew what I was supposed to do. The bundle was cloth, and it was going out to be sold, so it owed a toll going out, the same as the wick seller's wife's basket of wicks. I was supposed to tell her the toll, and take the copper, and make the mark, and give her a token. If she didn't have the copper, I was supposed to tell her to wait for daylight and come back when she did.

I looked over at the scarred guard. He was looking at the bundle. Then he looked at me.

"Go," I said to the woman.

She didn't move right away. I don't think she believed me.

"Go," I said again, and I tipped my head at the little door.

The scarred guard got up off the bench. He went over to the little door and lifted the bar off it and leaned it against the wall, and he pulled the door open, and the cool air from the road came in through it. He stood there holding it. He didn't look at the woman or the boy. He looked out the door at the dark.

The woman ducked through with the bundle catching on the top of the doorway, and she had to push it down to get under. The boy went after her. He looked back once, at the lamp in the niche, I think, not at me. Then they were out, and I heard their feet on the road for a little bit, and then I didn't.

The scarred guard shut the little door and put the bar back across it. He went and sat back down on the bench. His partner hadn't woken up.

I stood behind my counter and looked at the tablet. The wax was smooth. There was nothing on it for the day yet, and there wasn't going to be anything on it for her. I didn't make a mark. I didn't give a token. I put my stylus down in the groove and left it there.

Not long after that the gray came up, and the guards pulled the big doors back and propped them, and the first loads that had been waiting inside came up to my counter, the donkey man first, rubbing his eyes. I took his copper and made his mark. That was the first mark on the tablet that day.

The two men came at the fourth hour and stood in the shade the same as always.

A little after that, the man with the ink on his finger walked over to the post where the scarred guard was standing his day watch. He'd gone straight into it from the night one, because the post was short a man. I couldn't hear all of it. I heard the man with the ink ask if anything had come through early. I heard the scarred guard say, "Loads." The man with the ink asked something else, and the scarred guard said, "No," and then he said, "Ask the collector, it's his tablet," and he looked out at the road and didn't say anything else.

The man with the ink came over to my counter. He looked down at the tablet and read the marks, the donkey man and the loads after him, every one with its copper. He didn't ask me anything. He went back to the shade.

I looked over at the scarred guard. He didn't look back at me. He stood there with his arms at his sides in the bare dust where the onion skins used to be, and he watched the road.
