# Chapter Three

On the ninth day after they killed Stephen, the gate was slow, and I spent most of the afternoon watching the wasp. It had chewed so much mortar out of the seam over my counter by then that I could see a dark gap up there the width of my finger, and it kept going in and out of it with its little crumbs. The heat came off the wall into my shoulders the way it always did after midday. A man came through with a wicker cage of pigeons on his back, and one gray feather came loose and landed on the oil stain on my counter, and it stuck there in the old grease. He blew it off for me before he paid, and he laughed about it, and then he went on into the city with the birds cooing in the cage.

The scarred guard was standing on his onion skins at the post. They had dried out and gone thin as cloth by then, and he was still standing right on top of them. His partner was drinking from his skin. Neither one of them said anything to me all day, and I didn't say anything to them, which was how it had been since they came.

I don't know why it was that evening and not one of the others. Nothing happened at the gate to make it that night. I took the last load, which was a man with a cart of firewood, and I scratched his mark and locked the box and put the key in my pouch, the same as I had done the eight nights before. Then I started walking, and when I got to the lower market, I turned into the lane.

The market was closing up. Men were rolling up their awnings and tying them, and a woman was pouring a bucket of dirty water out into the gutter, and a boy was chasing a chicken that had gotten loose from a cage. The rope maker had his door shut and a bar across it. The lane went off behind his shop between two walls, and it was so narrow that I could have put a hand flat on each wall at the same time if I had wanted to. It smelled like old rope and cat. I walked down it slow, because the ground was uneven and it was getting dark, and up at the end I could see the yellow light coming out low under a door.

It was a low door, like she had said. It was set down a step below the lane, and the top of it came to about my chin. A young man was sitting on the step beside it with his back against the wall and his knees pulled up. He was maybe sixteen, with a thin face and a fuzzy start of a beard, and when he saw me coming down the lane, he stood up and put himself in front of the door.

He looked at me hard. Then he looked at my belt, where the pouch was, and back up at my face.

"You're the one from the northern gate," he said.

"Yes."

He didn't move right away. I could tell he was trying to decide something, and that he was scared, and that he had probably been told to sit out there and watch the lane and nobody had told him what to do about a toll collector. I didn't help him. I stood there in the lane and waited.

Then he stepped aside and pulled the door open for me.

I had to duck to get through it. I went down one more step on the inside, onto a dirt floor, and when I stood up straight, the room was full of people.

It was a storeroom, or it had been one. The ceiling was low and black from years of lamp smoke, and there were old pegs on the walls where things used to hang. There were maybe thirty people in there, sitting on the floor on mats and on their cloaks, packed in close enough that their knees were touching. Two clay lamps were burning on a shelf at the far end, and one more was sitting on the floor in the middle. It was hot in there with that many bodies, and it smelled like lamp oil and sweat and bread.

They all looked at me when I came in. Not all at once, but it went around the room the way water spreads on a floor, one face turning and then the one next to it. Someone near the back said something low to the woman beside him, and I didn't hear what it was, but I knew it was about me.

I didn't go in any farther. I put my back against the wall beside the door, and I stood there.

I knew some of them. I knew them the way I knew everyone in that city, from the gate and the market. There was a man who sold lamp wicks who came through my arch every couple of weeks. There was a woman who had come through with her husband and a cart of melons a month before. The woodworker was sitting about halfway back, with his big scarred hands resting on his knees. He saw me, and he didn't wave or call me over. He looked at me, and then he turned back around to the front.

The young woman with the basket wasn't there. I looked for her, and she wasn't anywhere in the room.

A little girl was sitting on the floor right by my feet, close to the door, where it was a little cooler. She was about five. Her mother was beside her with a baby asleep on her shoulder. The girl had a sandal on one foot that had broken across the strap at some point, and someone had tied the two ends together in a big knot to hold it on. It was a lumpy knot, too big for the strap, the kind someone ties fast in the street to get a child walking again. She kept picking at it with her fingers. Her mother reached down once without looking and moved her hand off of it, and a little later the girl started picking at it again.

At the front of the room an older man was sitting on a low stool. He had a loaf of bread in his lap, a round flat one, and a cup sitting on the ground beside his foot. He was talking low, the way a man tells his family at supper what happened that day.

He talked about Stephen. He said Stephen had carried food every morning to the widows who had nobody, and that there were women in that room who had eaten because of him. One of the women near the front put her face in her hands when he said that, and the woman next to her put an arm around her. He said Stephen had been buried. Some of the houses where they used to meet were being watched now, he said, and that was why they were down here. A few people said yes to that, low. I stood there by the door and thought about what I was. I was the man at the northern gate who wrote down everything that came through it, and whose count went to the farming rights every week, and they were letting me stand in their room with the door shut behind me.

He didn't say anything about the heavens. I waited for it, and he didn't say it.

Then he picked up the loaf and held it in both hands. He said the blessing over it, the same blessing my father used to say over bread, the same words, and everyone in the room said it along with him, low, and I didn't. My mouth stayed shut. I stood there against the wall and listened to thirty people say my father's words.

He broke the loaf in two. He said something more, something about the body, and I didn't follow all of it. Then he tore off a piece for himself and handed the rest to the man beside him, and it started going around the room.

It went slow. Every person who got it tore off a small piece and handed it on, and some of them ate their piece right away and some of them held onto it first. The loaf was getting smaller as it came. I watched it go across the front row and then come back along the second row, and then it came down the side of the room where I was standing.

The mother with the baby got it. She tore a piece for herself and a smaller piece for the girl, and the girl stopped picking at her knot long enough to eat it. Then the mother turned and held the rest of the loaf up toward me.

She didn't say anything. She held it there, about a hand's width from my fingers, and waited.

I didn't reach for it. My hands were down at my sides against the wall, and they stayed there. I don't know how long she held it. Long enough that her arm had to be getting tired, with the baby on her other shoulder. She didn't look at me like I had done something wrong. She looked at me like a woman looks at a man who is standing in the rain, when she knows she can't make him come inside.

Then she lowered it, and she turned and gave it to the old woman on her other side, and it went on around the room without me.

The cup came after it. It was a plain clay cup, the kind anyone's house would have, and it was full of wine. It went around the same way, hand to hand, every person taking one swallow and passing it, and some of them wiping the rim with their sleeve before they handed it on. When it got to the mother, she drank from it and gave the girl a tiny sip that made her face scrunch up, and then she held it up toward me the same way she had held the bread.

I could smell the wine from where I stood.

My hands stayed where they were. She waited, and then she handed it on.

After that they talked some more, and someone sang a psalm, low, so it wouldn't carry out into the lane, and a few people joined in. The girl at my feet leaned her head against her mother's leg and fell asleep with her fingers still on the knot in her sandal. I stood against the wall the whole time. My back was wet with sweat, and the wall was rough against it, and my legs were getting stiff, and I didn't sit down.

When it was over, people started getting up a few at a time and leaving. They didn't all go out at once. They went two or three together, and they waited a little while between each group, so the lane would never have a crowd in it. The young man on the step put his head in each time to say when the lane was clear. Some of the people who went past me to get to the door looked at me, and some didn't. The man who sold lamp wicks went by and kept his eyes on the floor.

The woodworker came over when the room was about half empty. He stood in front of me. He was shorter than I was, and he had to tip his head back a little to look at my face.

"You came," he said.

"I came," I said.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't ask me if I would come back, or what I thought of it, or why I hadn't taken the bread. He put one of those big hands on my shoulder for about the length of a breath, and it was heavy and warm through my tunic, and then he took it off and ducked out the low door into the lane.

I waited for the next group to go, and then I went too.

The young man was sitting on the step again. He didn't stand up this time. He looked at me as I came up out of the doorway, and then he looked back down the lane, and I went past him.

It was full dark by then. The lane was black between the walls, and I had to put my hand on the rope maker's wall to find the turn. Out in the market street there was a little moonlight, and the stalls were all shut, and a dog was asleep in a doorway. I walked home through the city to the dye works. The yard was empty, and the vats were sitting there under the moon with their covers on. I went up my stair.

I didn't believe anything walking home that I hadn't believed walking down the lane. I hadn't taken the bread or the cup, and I hadn't said the blessing. What I had done was walk. I had walked down that lane and stood against that wall for most of the night, and then I had walked home again. My feet had gone somewhere they hadn't gone before, and the rest of me had gone along with them. That was all it was, and it was the first step.

My room was hot and dark. The fig was still on the sill, and the ants had gone off it. The lamp was on the shelf, and I didn't light it. I didn't lie down right away either. I stood at the window with the shutter open, and the bottoms of my feet were gritty from the lane. There was dust on my sandal straps, and it was gray, a different color from the dust at the gate.

When I put my sleeve up to my face, I could smell that low room in the cloth, the bread and the lamp oil and all those people packed in close together. I stood there at the window smelling it until the last lamp in the houses across the yard had gone out.
