# Chapter Eight

They opened the lamp shop the same night I took the man's wrist.

I locked up when the light started to go, and I walked home through the lower market. When I got to my own turn, I could hear it from down the street of the potters. It wasn't loud. It was the sound of a lot of men standing in one place and a few of them giving orders, and the neighbors talking low in their doorways, and every so often somebody hitting wood. I didn't go to my turn. I went on down the street of the potters, past the jars with their ropes on them, and I stopped at the top of the potters' steps with some people who were already standing there looking down.

There were torches at the bottom. There were six or seven men from the Temple guard, and two of them had a heavy bar of wood between them, and they were swinging it into the door of the lamp shop. It took them four tries. An old potter standing next to me, with clay still dried gray on his forearms, said low, to nobody, "That's the third door on this street." Nobody answered him. On the fourth one the door went in and hung off its top hinge, and a puff of clay dust came out into the torchlight.

The man with the ink on his finger was standing off to one side with his tablet in its case. He had his right sleeve pulled down long, all the way over his hand.

Then the young man from the coats came down the steps.

He came down right past me. I could have reached out and touched his cloak. He didn't look at any of us. He went down the steps quick and light, like a man with somewhere to be, and at the bottom he didn't wait for the guards to go in first. He went in first himself. The door was low, and he ducked his head under the top of it, and he took the bottom of his cloak up in one hand so it wouldn't drag in the dust, and he went into that dark room with a guard behind him holding up a torch.

He wasn't in there long. When he came out, he was still holding his cloak up off the ground. One of the guards came out after him with the cloth that had been hung over the shutter, and another one came out with the two clay lamps, one in each hand, and set them down in the street. They were cold. Nobody had lit them since the night before. The young man from the coats looked at the lamps on the ground, and then he said something short to the man with the ink, and the man with the ink nodded.

There wasn't anybody in there. They had kept their word about not coming back.

I thought that was the end of it. The guards started to come back up the steps with their torches, and the people around me started to move off. But the young man from the coats didn't come back up. He walked along the bottom of the steps to the third house down, a little house with a blue door, and he stood in front of it and waited while one of the guards knocked. Nobody came. The guard knocked again. Then the young man from the coats put his own hand flat on the door and pushed it, and it wasn't barred, and it swung in. He went in through it himself, and the guards went in after him.

They brought her out a little after that.

I knew her face. She had been in both rooms, the storeroom by the lane and the lamp shop, and I had never said a word to her or heard her say one. In the storeroom she had sat near the front, and when the man on the stool talked about the widows, she had put her face in her hands. She was older than I'd thought. She was barefoot, the way a woman would be in her own house at night when somebody came in, and she had a shawl around her shoulders that somebody must have handed her on the way out. They had tied her wrists in front of her with the same kind of cord they'd used on the woodworker.

She didn't make a sound. A girl about fifteen came running out of the house after her, crying, and one of the guards caught her and held her back by the arm. The woman turned around and said something to the girl, low, and the girl stopped pulling.

The young man from the coats came out last. He pulled the blue door shut behind him, carefully, the way a man shuts his own door. Then he walked up the steps at the front of them all, and they brought the woman up behind him, and he went right past me again, close enough that I could smell the smoke from the torches on his cloak. I didn't move. I stood there with my hands at my sides.

When they were gone, the neighbors took the girl inside somewhere. I stood at the top of the steps until the street was empty.

Then I went down.

The lamp shop door was still hanging off its top hinge. There was a little light coming in from a lamp in a window across the way, enough that I could see the room once my eyes got used to it. It was the room from the night before, with the empty shelves and the broken lamps, but now there were footprints all through the clay dust on the floor, big ones from the guards' sandals going everywhere. I stood just inside the door, about where I'd stood the night before.

On the bottom shelf, on the far side, there was a broken lamp lying on its side, and there was something white under it. I went over and picked up the lamp, and under it there was a scrap of papyrus folded over twice.

I took it to the doorway where the light was and opened it. It was a list. There were seven names on it in charcoal, written small, the way the younger man would have written them down to say them out loud. I could read them. I'd been reading names on contracts and on the clerk's board for four years. The woodworker's name was the third one down, and I knew it now, because I'd heard it said in that room. I didn't know the other six. My name wasn't on it.

I read it twice to be sure. Then I folded it back up the way it had been and put it in my belt, in with my own coppers, and I walked home.

I didn't sleep much. I lay on the cot with my belt still on, and every time I turned over I could feel the papyrus crackle in it against my side. Down in the dye works yard somebody had left a vat uncovered, and the smell came up through the window all night, sour and strong.

The woodworker came through the arch the next morning, a little after first light.

He came in from the north road, on foot, by himself. At first I didn't know him. He was walking slow, with one hand pressed against his side under his arm, the way a man walks when his ribs hurt every time he takes a breath. The whole left side of his face was one big bruise. It had gone yellow and green around the edges and was still dark in the middle, and his left eye was swollen most of the way shut. The cut over it had closed up into a black line.

He stopped at my counter. He didn't have anything to toll. He put his hand down flat on the wood, his hand with the two black fingernails, and leaned on it a little. There was a red line all the way around his wrist where the cord had been.

"They let me go," he said. "Two days ago, right before they shut the gates. Some of us, not all." He breathed for a second. "I went out by the Sheep Gate so nobody I knew would see me, out to my brother's place up the road. I didn't want her to see my face like this. It didn't help any."

"She's all right," I said. "Your wife."

"I know she is." He looked at me with the eye that would open. "She told my brother's boy, and he told me. She said you came to the lamp shop."

"I did."

He nodded. He stood there leaning on his hand, getting his breath. I took my water skin down off its peg under the counter and held it out to him. He looked at it, and then he took it and drank with his head tipped back and his eyes shut, and he handed it back to me without saying anything about it.

"I'm going to finish that chair," he said. "The glue went hard on me. I have to clean it out of the joints and make new." He breathed again. "Then we're going."

"Going where?"

He didn't answer that. He took his hand off my counter and straightened up as much as his side would let him, and he went in under the arch and up the street toward the lower market, and the people waiting behind him with their loads stepped back out of his way without anybody telling them to. He went slow, with his hand pressed against his ribs. I watched him until he went around the corner.

The man with the ink on his finger was in the shade of the arch at the fourth hour with the big man in the brown cloak. He had his sleeve pulled down over his right hand again. Once, around midday, he reached up to rub his eye, and the sleeve fell back, and I saw his wrist. The red marks had gone dark overnight. There were four of them on top, purple, about the size of the end of my fingers, and I knew there was one more underneath. He saw me looking. He pulled the sleeve back down.

The rest of the day went the way the days had been going. A man brought in salted fish in baskets, and a woman brought in flax, and two men brought in a load of roof beams on their shoulders and argued about who was carrying more of it. "You've got the light end," the one in front said. "There isn't a light end," the one behind said. "It's a beam." They set it down on the street to pay me, both of them breathing hard, and then picked it up and kept arguing all the way in under the arch. The count was small. I took the coppers and made the marks.

At the ninth hour a boy from the offices came out to my counter. It was the boy from before, still in the tunic that was too long for him.

"You're to come to the offices," he said. "When you lock up."

I went when the light started to go. I locked the box and put the leftover tokens in my pouch, and the key in after them, and I tied the pouch shut and walked up into the city. The doorkeeper was sitting on his stool inside the door with a pomegranate split open in his lap. He was eating the seeds out of it one at a time with his fingers, and spitting the little bits of white pith into a pot by his foot, and the juice had run down his wrist and he kept licking it off. He didn't get up. He pointed at the far end of the room with his chin.

I had to wait. There was a man at the table ahead of me, a merchant by his clothes, arguing about a tax on some oil he'd brought in by the south road, and the older man with the ring let him talk until he was done and then told him the same number again. The merchant went out past me red in the face.

It was the older man with the ring again, at his table at the far end. He had a board out, and I could see the marks from before on it, my name and my father's name and where I slept.

"You haven't brought the crossing," he said.

"There's no mark."

"I know there's no mark." He wrote something under my name. "The offices will give you one more day to put it right. The toll and the mark, and who let her through. After that it goes to Saul's board, and it's out of our hands." He said it the way a man says the name of the street he lives on. He didn't look up, and the men at the tables near us didn't look up either. They all knew whose board it was.

"Saul's man tells me you put your hand on him," he said.

"He reached for my pouch."

He wrote that down too. Then he closed the board. "One more day," he said.

I walked out past the doorkeeper and his pomegranate and down the steps into the street.

I knew whose man the man with the ink was. I had watched him show his tablet to the young man from the coats at the end of the woodworker's street, and I had watched him stand by the night before while the young man from the coats went through two doors. So that was his name. Saul. I said it once to myself, walking, to hear it.

I went home through the lower market. When I came to the woodworker's shop, there was a lamp lit inside, and the door with the leather strip on its hinge was pulled almost shut. I could hear a mallet. It was going slow, a few taps, and then a stop, and then a few taps again, the way a man works when he has to rest between them. I stood in the street and listened to it until it stopped for longer than the other times. Then it started again, and I went on home.
