# Chapter Two

The iron box was chained to the front leg of my counter, and the chain was still cool from the night and wet with dew when I pulled it around to get at the lock. I put the key in and turned it. The lid came up the way it came up every morning, heavy and a little stiff on the left hinge, and the coppers from the day before were still lying in the bottom of it where I had left them. I set the tablet back in its groove. Yesterday's marks were still in the wax, and the last one was the barley man's, with the woman's goat and the two brothers with the reeds after it.

The gate opened at first light the same as any other morning. The guards pulled the big doors back and propped them, and the first loads that had been waiting outside on the road all night started coming through under the arch. There was a man with a string of camels carrying salt, and behind him a farmer with two baskets of cucumbers hanging off a pole, and behind him a woman with a donkey that wouldn't walk over the cracked paving stone until she slapped it on the rump. Each wheel and hoof that went over that stone made its little knock, and I counted them.

I had slept some. I had gotten up in the dark and come to the gate the long way around, so I wouldn't walk past the council hall, and the fig was still sitting on the windowsill back in my room where I had left it.

The older guard came over to my counter a little after the salt man had gone through. He stood there rubbing his knee before he said anything.

"His people came for him," he said. "The night watch said they stayed out there with him all night, and before first light they carried him off down the road toward the tombs on a board with a cloth over him. They were crying the whole way, loud enough that the watch on the wall came down the stairs to see what it was." He looked at the road. "They took him to bury him. I thought you'd want to know where he went."

"Thank you," I said.

He nodded once and went back to his post, and he didn't bring it up again.

From my counter I could see a little of the open ground off to the left, past the date seller's mat. It looked the way it always looked, rubble and thistle and broken roof tile, except there was one patch near the middle where the dirt was darker than the dirt around it. I looked at it until a man with a cart of firewood came up to the counter, and then I looked at him.

After that it went back to being ordinary days at the gate.

It was the same work every day, and I did it. Open the box, set the tablet, take the coppers, hand out the tokens, scratch the marks, and lock up when the light went. The wasp was still working the seam in the roof over my head. Some mornings it was already there before I was, and some mornings it didn't show up until the heat came, but every day it was taking a little more of that old mortar out of the wall, one crumb at a time.

There was an oil stain on the front of my counter, right where people leaned their arms while they dug in their purses. It was about the size of my hand, and it had been there since before my contract started, from a jar someone had set down there with a crack in it. It had soaked down into the grain and gone dark. When I first took the counter I had meant to sand it out, and I never got around to it. Most days I didn't see it at all. That week I kept noticing it, with people's elbows sitting right on top of it.

On the second day the copper seller came through. He came up from the north every couple of weeks with a donkey loaded down with pots and pans and ladles, all of them tied on with rope so they rattled against each other on every step, and anyone on the road could hear him coming a long way off. He was a big friendly man with a voice that carried, and he liked to talk.

"Collector," he said, and slapped the counter. "How's the box this morning?"

"Same as always," I said. "That's three coppers for the load."

"Three coppers to bring copper into the city. Every time I pay you I lose money on my own goods." He laughed at that like he always did, and counted the coins out onto the oil stain. "You were here for it, weren't you? The one they took outside the wall?"

I picked up his coppers and put them in the box.

"Out on the road they're already telling it," he said. "They say he looked up at the sky and told them he could see it open. That he said it right to their faces, and that's what did it." He shook his head. "A man says a thing like that in front of the council, he knows what he's buying."

I gave him his token. "Your donkey's chewing on my post."

He laughed again and pulled the donkey off the post, and he went on into the city with his pans knocking, and I could hear him for a long way down the street.

On the third day the guards changed. The older guard and the younger one were sent to some other post, and nobody told me where. The new pair were men I had never sat across from before. One of them was tall and drank from his skin a lot and didn't offer it to anyone. The other one had a scar that ran down through his left eyebrow and split it in two, and he watched the tablet in my groove the way a man watches a dog he doesn't know. A farmer had come in that morning with onions, and the dry skins had blown off the load and collected in the dust by the post, and the scarred guard stood right on top of them all day without ever kicking them out of the way.

On the fourth day some men came out from the city with a cart, and they spent most of the afternoon walking around on the open ground picking up rocks. They were taking the good flat ones, the ones a man could build with, and stacking them in the cart, and when the cart was full they brought it back in through the arch. I asked the driver where they were going.

"New wall on a house up past the pool," he said. "Why buy stone when it's lying out there for free?"

He didn't owe a toll, because he was bringing the rocks in from the city's own ground, so I waved him through. The wheels knocked going over the cracked stone, and I counted the knock, even though there wasn't a mark to go with it.

A potter's boy came through that same afternoon behind his father's donkey, carrying a little cage made out of reeds with a cricket inside it. The cricket started singing right under the arch, where the stone walls made it loud, and the boy stopped and held the cage up to his ear and grinned, and his father yelled at him to keep up.

On the fifth day the clerk from the farming rights came by with his board and a stick of charcoal and asked for the week's count. I unlocked the box and let him count the coppers out on the counter, and he ran his finger down the marks in my wax and made his own marks on his board to match. The numbers came out even, the way they always did. He didn't ask about the afternoon when the crowd had gone out through the gate, and I didn't tell him. The farming rights wanted the number, and the number was right.

On the sixth day it was the Sabbath, so the gate stayed shut to loads and I didn't go down to it. I stayed in my room most of the day. The dye works was shut too, and without the dyers banging around in the yard I could hear the families in the houses on either side, the children running on the roofs and someone's grandmother calling them down. In the afternoon a man next door was saying the psalms out loud, slow, the way a father says them so his boys can learn them. I didn't go up to the Temple. I sat on the cot with my back against the wall and listened to him until he stopped.

On the seventh day the woodworker came.

He came in a little before midday with a donkey carrying a bundle of cut boards, olive and some cedar, tied on both sides so the donkey walked a little wide. He was an older man with a short gray beard. When he put his hand down on the counter, I noticed his hands first. They were thick and brown, with pale little scars all over the backs of them and two fingernails that had gone black from being hit with something, and the skin on his palms was so hard it scraped on the wood.

"Two coppers for the boards," I said.

He paid it. Then he didn't move on. He stood there with his hand on the counter while the donkey shifted its feet behind him.

"You're Eli," he said.

I didn't say yes or no. I held his token out to him.

"Stephen talked about you," he said. He took the token but he kept talking. "He said he grew up with a man who sat at the northern gate, and that the man could argue all night long on a rooftop and still be at his counter at first light. He said you never once let him win."

"He didn't need me to let him."

The woodworker almost smiled at that. Then he lowered his voice, though nobody at the post was close enough to hear him. "Some of us eat together in the evenings. It's a few of us, and there's always bread. You'd be welcome to come sit with us."

I looked past him at the road, where a man with a cart of melons was waiting in the sun.

"I collect the tolls," I said.

"I know what you collect." He put the token away in his belt. "I'm not asking you about that."

He took his donkey by the rope and led it on through the arch, and the boards rocked a little on its sides, and I watched his back until he went around the corner. Then I waved the melon man up and charged him for his melons.

On the eighth day a young woman with a basket came.

It was around the sixth hour, and the heat was coming up off the street, and she came in on foot from the north road carrying a basket on her hip with a cloth tied down over the top of it. She was young, younger than me by a good bit, and she had dust on her feet up to the ankles from walking. She set a copper on the counter for the bread before I even asked for it. I picked it up and held out her token.

She didn't take it right away. She looked right at me, and I could tell she had come to say something.

"He spoke of you before they took him," she said. "The ones who loved him meet in the evenings near the lower market. There's a low door in the lane behind the rope maker's, and you'll have to duck to go through it. It's after the lamps are lit, any night. Nobody will ask you anything."

She took the token out of my hand and turned around and walked into the city with her basket, and she didn't look back.

"I collect the tolls," I said. She was already under the arch by then, and she didn't hear me, or if she did hear me she didn't stop.

The scarred guard was watching me from the post with his feet on the onion skins. I didn't look at him. I took the next load, which was a man with a sack of lentils, and the next one after that, and I kept taking them until the light went orange and then gray and the guards started pulling the doors shut.

I locked the box and put the key in my pouch, and I started home.

The way home took me right through the lower market, the same as it did every night. Most of the stalls were closed up by then, and the men were taking down their awnings and the women were sweeping out under them, and somewhere a baker was pulling the last loaves out of his oven, because the whole street smelled like it. The rope maker's shop was just past the oil sellers. Up at the end of the narrow lane behind it, there was a little bit of yellow light coming out low near the ground, where someone had a lamp lit behind a door.

I didn't turn into the lane. I walked past it at the same pace I had been walking, and I didn't slow down until I was past the oil sellers and the market was behind me.

Back at the dye works, the dyers had gone home and the yard was empty. I went up the outside stair and opened my door. The fig was still sitting on the windowsill where I had put it the first night, and it had gone dark and soft in the heat, and a couple of ants had found it and were walking around on the skin. The skin was still whole. I left it there.

The basin still had its skin of dust on top. The lamp was still sitting on the shelf, full of oil, with the flint next to it.

I sat down on the cot in the dark and took off my sandals, and I set them side by side on the floor next to the cot where I could find them in the morning.
