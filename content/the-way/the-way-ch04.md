# Chapter Four

On the tenth morning, people started going out through my gate with everything they owned. The first one I noticed was a man with a bedroll and two cooking pots tied to his back, and his wife behind him with a baby, and three more children behind her carrying bundles. Traders carry goods, and these people were carrying their houses. Nobody owed a toll going out unless they were taking goods to sell, so I didn't stop them. I watched them go out under the arch and up the north road.

By midday I had seen maybe six families do it. Most of them didn't look at me. One woman looked at me hard, right at my face and then at the tablet in its groove, and then she pulled her little boy closer to her side and walked faster.

The lamp-wick seller came through a little after that. He wasn't coming in with his tray of wicks the way he did every couple of weeks. He was going out, with his wife, and he had a sack over his shoulder and a rolled-up mat under his arm. He stopped at my counter because his wife had a basket of wicks to sell up the road, and that owed a copper. He counted it out onto the counter with his fingers shaking so bad that the copper rolled off the edge, and he had to bend down and pick it up out of the dust. He didn't look at me once. He set the copper down again, took his token, and went out under the arch with his eyes on the ground and his wife holding onto his sleeve.

The scarred guard watched him go. Then he looked at me, and he didn't say anything.

Up in the seam over my head, the wasp had stopped carrying mortar out. It was carrying something in now, little gray bits I couldn't make out, and going into the gap it had dug and staying in there longer each time. I watched it go in and come out a few times between loads. Whatever it was building, it was building it inside my wall.

A cart came in after that with one new wheel and one old one. The new wheel was pale, fresh wood, and the old one was gray and cracked along the rim, and the whole cart squeaked on the new side every time the wheel came around. The driver said he'd had it made in the spring and it still hadn't stopped squeaking. I charged him for his load of jars and he went on in, squeaking all the way up the street, and I could still hear it after he had turned the corner.

The man with the ink on his finger came in the afternoon.

He came out from the city, not in from the road, and he didn't have any goods with him. He was a thin man about my age, with a neat beard and a clean tunic, and he carried a wax tablet in a leather case on a strap over his shoulder. The first finger of his right hand was stained dark with ink all the way up to the second knuckle, the way a man's finger gets when he writes on papyrus all day and wipes his pen on his own skin. He put that hand flat on my counter, right on the oil stain.

"You keep the count at this gate," he said.

"I collect the tolls."

"Who's been going out this morning? With bedding, and cooking pots, and children."

"People," I said.

"Names."

"I don't write names. I write loads." I tipped my head at the tablet in the groove. "A mark is a copper. Nobody going out owes a copper unless they're carrying goods to sell."

He leaned over and read down the marks on my tablet like they might tell him something if he looked at them long enough. They didn't, because there wasn't anything in them but loads and coppers. He straightened up.

"You knew the one they took outside the wall," he said. "Stephen."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't the way the older guard had said it either. The older guard had said it like a man setting something down gently. This man said it like he was writing it.

"I collect the tolls," I said.

"I know what you collect." He took his hand off my counter. There was a faint smudge of ink left on the edge of the oil stain where his finger had been. "We'll come by again."

He went back into the city. I looked at the smudge, and then I rubbed it off with my thumb, and my thumb came away gray.

On the eleventh day they opened the woodworker's house.

I didn't know it was his house until I got there. I had walked past that shop every night for years on my way home, down the street that comes out of the lower market toward the dye works, and I had never once known whose it was. It was a low front with a wide door, and there was always sawdust out on the step and a smell of cut cedar coming out of it. I had probably heard him working in there a hundred times.

That evening there were people standing in the street in front of it. They were neighbors, mostly, women with their arms crossed and men standing back against the walls across the way, and nobody was talking. The wide door was hanging open with one of its hinges torn out of the frame. Inside I could see his tools all over the floor where someone had swept them off the walls, the chisels and the mallets and the saws, and a big plane lying upside down in the sawdust.

Then they brought him out.

There were four men with him, men from the Temple guard by the look of them, and two of them had him by the arms. They had tied his wrists in front of him with a cord. He had a cut over one eye that was still bleeding, and he was walking slow, the way an older man walks when he's been knocked down and gotten back up. His wife came out behind them crying, and one of the guards put his arm out and stopped her in the doorway.

The man with the ink on his finger came out last. He had his tablet out of its case, and he was writing on it as he walked. He stopped in the street and looked around at the neighbors, one face at a time, and wrote something else.

And at the far end of the street, standing by himself where it opened out into the market, was the young man who had stood by the coats.

I knew him the second I saw him. He was in the same kind of clean cloak, and he had his arms folded the same way, and he was watching them lead the woodworker down the street toward him the same way he had watched them choose their rocks. He didn't come any closer. When they got to him, he said something short to the man with the ink, and the man with the ink nodded and showed him the tablet, and then they all turned the corner together and went off toward the upper city.

The woodworker never looked at me. I was standing back against a wall with the neighbors, and he walked right past me with his tied hands out in front of him, and he kept his eyes on the street. I've thought about that. I don't know if he didn't see me, or if he saw me and kept his eyes down so nobody would follow his look to my face.

After they were gone, his wife sat down on the step in the sawdust and put her face in her apron. Two of the neighbor women went and sat with her. I went up to the doorway.

On the bench just inside the door, there was a chair he had been making. It had three of its legs in already, fitted tight into the seat, and the fourth leg was lying on the bench next to it. That leg was finished and smooth, ready to go in, and there was a little pot of glue beside it with a stick in it, and the glue had gone hard. He had been about to put that last leg in when they came.

I stood there and looked at that chair. Four days before, he had stood at my counter with his hand on the wood and told me there was always bread, and that he wasn't asking me about what I collected. He had gone home after that and worked on this chair. Then one of the neighbor women looked up at me from the step, and I went home.

On the twelfth day the clerk came for the week's count.

He came at the same hour he always came, with his board and his stick of charcoal, and I unlocked the box and let him count the coppers out on the counter. He ran his finger down my marks and made his own on the board. The number was right. It was always right.

Then he didn't close the board. He stood there with the charcoal in his fingers.

"Somebody asked me about your evenings," he said.

"Who?"

"Somebody at the offices." He looked at the board instead of at me. "They asked if the collector at the northern gate goes straight home when he locks up. I said I didn't know where you go when you lock up. That's true. I don't." He made a little mark on the edge of his board, off to the side away from the numbers, and I watched him do it. "But I'm writing down that they asked."

"What do they want to know for?"

"They didn't say. They don't have to say." He closed the board. "Your number is right. It's been right every week for four years. That's what the farming rights care about, and as long as it stays right, I can tell them that." He tucked the charcoal into his belt. "Keep the number right, and keep your evenings dull. That's all I've got for you."

He went back into the city. I didn't know whether he had done me a kindness or not, and I still don't.

Late that afternoon the door boy came through the arch.

He came in from the road, on foot and dusty, and at first I didn't know him without the low door behind him and the step under him. He stopped at my counter and didn't put down any copper. He was breathing hard, like he had been walking fast.

"Don't come down the lane," he said, low. "Not behind the rope maker's. They know about the door."

"Who knows?"

"The ones with the lists. When they took a man this morning, his wife heard them reading off their list. Our lane was on it, and the door, and the step." He looked right at my face while he said it, and he kept looking, and he didn't say anything for a breath. I knew what he was looking for. I knew what all of them were thinking about the man from the northern gate who had stood by their door three nights ago, and I didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything I could say that would have done any good.

"Nobody's going there now," he said. "It's dark. If anybody comes down that lane now, they'll take him."

"Where did they go?"

He shook his head. He wasn't going to tell me that, and I didn't blame him.

"The woman with the basket said to tell you," he said. "So I told you." He turned around and walked off into the city without paying anything, because he hadn't brought anything in.

That evening I locked up with the light going and the Sabbath coming in, and I walked home through the lower market, the way I always went.

I didn't go down the lane. I stopped across the street from the rope maker's, in the doorway of an oil seller who had already shut up for the night, and I stood there in the dark of his doorway where I could see the mouth of the lane. The rope maker had his bar across his door. The lane went back between the walls the way it always did, and down at the end of it there wasn't any light. Three nights before there had been yellow light coming out low under that door. Now it was just dark all the way down.

I stood there a long time. The market was empty, and the awnings were all rolled up and tied, and the gutter down the middle of the street still smelled like the fish sellers' water from that morning. Up and down the street the Sabbath lamps were burning in the windows, and down at the end of the lane there was one window with no lamp in it. Once a dog came out of the lane and trotted off down the street.

Then I saw that I wasn't the only one watching it.

There was a man sitting on a step farther down the street, on the same side as the lane, with his back against a wall and his knees up, the way the door boy used to sit. He wasn't sleeping. Every so often he turned his head and looked down the lane, and then he looked up and down the street, and then he went back to sitting. He was too far off to see his face. He never looked over at the oil seller's doorway. I don't think he knew I was there.

I stood there until my legs were stiff. Nobody went down the lane. Nobody came out of it.

Then I stepped out of the doorway and walked home along the dark side of the street. I went past the woodworker's shop. The door had been pulled shut, as much as it would shut with the hinge torn out, and there was a rag stuffed in the gap to keep it from swinging. There wasn't any sawdust on the step anymore. Someone had swept it.
