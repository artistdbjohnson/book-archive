# Chapter Sixteen

We went down the woman's ladder before it was light on the first day of the week, the thirty-fifth day, and she was up already, sitting in her doorway, and she watched us go and didn't say anything. He thanked her. She nodded at him.

The road went north out of that village along the side of a long valley. It was cooler early, and he went better than he had, with the day's rest in him, and for the first hour he only stopped twice. She had the big bundle on her back and the small one in her hand, the way she'd had them since the city, and I had the skin. I'd put the strip of cloth back in under my strap, against the heel, and it held.

We didn't talk much. When we did, it was the road.

The valley was all fields on both sides, cut and empty, with stone walls round them and little stone huts in the corners for the watchmen at harvest, all empty now. A man was out early along one of the field walls, cutting the thorn brush back off it with a hook and stacking it in a pile, and he stopped and leaned on the hook and watched us go by. There was dew on the scrub at the side of the road, and our feet got wet from it. Then the sun came over the hill and it was gone.

About the second hour the valley opened out, and there was a town on a hill ahead of us, a real town, with a wall round the top of the hill and the houses packed in behind it and spilling down outside it on the slope below. The road went up to it and in through a gate in the wall and out the other side, I suppose, because there wasn't any other way round that I could see.

"We'll have to go through," he said.

"Do they take a toll?" she said.

"Not on feet," I said.

She looked at me. Then she looked back at the town.

It took us most of the morning to get up to it, because the road went back and forth across the slope to make the climb easier for animals, and every turn of it was in the sun. The houses outside the wall were poor ones, mud and stones, with goats tied in the doorways and children sitting in the dust. In one doorway an old woman sat spinning, the spindle dropping and turning and climbing back up her thread, and she didn't look up at any of us going by.

Then we came round the last turn and the gate was in front of us.

It was smaller than mine. It was an arch in the wall, not much more than twice the height of a man, with a pair of old wooden doors folded back against the stone inside it, and a stone bench running along the inside of the arch on one side, in the shade. On the other side, back in the shade of the arch where the loads came through, there was a counter. It was a plank on two stone posts, with an iron box on it chained to one of the posts, and a wax tablet lying beside the box, and a stool behind it.

There was a man behind the counter. He was about my age, maybe a little younger, with a round face and a good tunic, and he was taking a toll from a man with a donkey loaded with charcoal. I watched him do it. He looked at the load, and he said the price, and the man said it was too much, and he said it again, the same price, and the man paid him. He opened the box and dropped the copper in and closed it. He made a mark on the tablet. He gave the man a token. It was clay.

I stood in the road and watched him do all that, and I didn't know I'd stopped until she touched my arm.

There were two town watchmen on the gate, with staves and no spears, sitting on the stone bench in the shade. They looked at everybody coming in, but they didn't stop most of them. When we came up, one of them got up and stood in the way. He wasn't rough about it. He stood there and looked at the three of us.

"Where from?"

"Jerusalem," she said.

"Where to?"

"North," she said. "His brother's people."

He looked at the woodworker, at the way he stood, and at his face. He looked at me. He looked at my belt, and he didn't seem to see anything there to ask about.

"There's a lot of you," he said. "Coming up from there."

None of us said anything. He moved out of the way and sat back down on the bench.

The man at the counter didn't look up at us. We owed nothing. We came through the arch past him, close enough that I could see the marks on his tablet, rows of them in a groove of wax, the same as mine, and the iron box, and the chain. The box was newer than mine. The chain was the same.

Inside the gate there was a little open place with a well and some stalls round it, and a market street going off it up the hill. It was market day, the first day of the week, and the street was full. There were people selling oil and cloth and pots and cheese, and a man with a brazier roasting something on sticks that smelled so good I had to stop.

"Sit down," she said to him. "Over there. I'll find bread."

There was a step in the shade against the side of a house, and he sat down on it, slow, with his hand on his side. She put the big bundle down beside him and went off up the market street with the small one.

"I'll go," I said. "I've got copper."

She stopped and looked back at me.

"I'll get it," I said. "Stay with him."

She didn't argue. She came back and sat down on the step next to him, with the bundle between her feet, and I went up the street.

There were three bakers along that street, and the first two had sold out. The third was a woman with a flat basket of loaves on a trestle in front of her door, round flat barley loaves, with only five or six left in the bottom of it.

"How much?"

"A copper for two."

I put my hand in my belt and got one out. I hadn't had any of them out since I'd left. I held it out to her on my palm, and I looked at it there.

It was one of mine. The clerk had paid it to me with my wages some week or other, out of his bag, and I didn't know which week. There was grit on it. There was gray grit worked into the stamp of it and down round the edge, the gray grit off the road under the arch, that got into everything there, into the cracks of the counter and the seams of the box and the creases of my hands. I'd been handling coppers with that grit on them every day for four years, and I'd never once looked at it.

She took it off my hand. She didn't look at it. She dropped it in a bowl with the others and gave me two loaves.

So that left eight.

I carried the loaves back down the street, one in each hand. They were still a little warm on the bottom. When I got back to the step, he was sitting with his eyes shut and his back against the wall of the house, and she was watching the gate.

I gave her one of the loaves and kept the other. She looked at it and then at me.

"One's yours," I said. "The two of you."

She put it in the small bundle, wrapped in the cloth, for later.

I went and stood by the well with my loaf in my hand. I had a drink out of the skin, and I filled it from the well, hauling the bucket up hand over hand on a wet rope that had worn a deep groove into the stone lip of the well, and I stood there with my back to them and looked at the gate.

From inside, I could see the counter side on, the man on his stool, the box, the chain round the post. A load came through, oil jars on a donkey, and he did it all over again. He looked, said the price, took the copper, dropped it in, closed the box, made the mark, gave the token. He was quick at it. He'd done it a lot of times.

The next one was a man with two baskets of cheese, and the collector knew him. They talked a little, and laughed, and the man paid, and the collector gave him a token, and I watched him make the mark. He made it smaller than the others. I didn't know if that meant anything at his gate. At mine it would have.

Then, a little after, the road outside went empty, and he got up off his stool and stretched, and said something to the watchmen on the bench, and one of them laughed. He went off round the side of the arch, through a little door in the wall there, and didn't come back.

Nobody was on the stool now.

I don't know how long I stood by the well looking at it. My feet hurt. The heel had opened again under the cloth on the climb, and the cloth had slipped down and balled up under my foot, and every step on it had been like walking on a pebble. My back hurt from the wall and the ground and the roof, three nights, and my legs hurt from the hills. The stool was in the shade of the arch. It was a plain wooden stool, the seat worn smooth and a little hollow in the middle where men had sat on it for years.

I went over to it. I walked back across the open place and in under the edge of the arch to the end of the counter, and I stood next to the stool, and I put my hand on the counter. The plank was rough and warm. There was a knot in it, near the edge, that somebody had rubbed smooth.

The watchmen on the bench were looking at me. Neither one of them said anything. Out on the road beyond the arch there wasn't a load in sight, only the heat coming up off the slope, and a long way down it somebody small walking up, too far off to see if they had anything with them.

I stood there. I could feel exactly how it would be to sit down on that stool and put my feet flat and my back against the stone of the arch, in the shade, and wait for the next load, and look at it, and say the price. I knew how it would feel in my legs. I knew how the box would sound when the lid came down.

I didn't sit down.

I took my hand off the counter. I stood there another breath. Then I turned round and walked back across the open place to the step where the two of them were sitting.

She had seen all of it. I could tell by her face. She didn't say anything about it, and he had his eyes shut, and I sat down on the end of the step next to her, in the shade of the house, on the stone.

After a little the man came back out of the little door, wiping his mouth, and sat down on his stool, and the next load came in.

We sat there until the worst of the heat was over. People came and went at the well. A man filled his jar and stood drinking out of the dipper and looking at us, and then went away. Two girls came down the street with water jars on their hips and stopped at the well and talked about somebody's wedding until their mother shouted down the street for them. The stalls started to close up for the middle of the day, the awnings coming down one after another, and the street went empty. I ate my loaf there on the step, the whole thing, slowly, and drank out of the skin. It was good bread. It was dry and a little burned on the bottom and I ate every crumb of it, and then I sat with my hands on my knees.

When the sun had gone round off the street, he said, "All right," and she helped him up, the two tries it always took now, and she put the bundle on her back.

We went up the market street and over the top of the hill through the town and down to the gate on the far side, which didn't have any counter at all, only a watchman asleep on a bench, and out through it onto the road north. The road went down the hill on that side in the late sun, back and forth, and at the bottom it ran along the next valley between the fields.

There were more people on the road on that side, coming up toward the town to get in before the gate shut, and a few going down ahead of us the way we were going. A man and his grown son passed us with packs on their backs, walking fast, and the son looked at the woodworker and said something to his father, and the father shook his head at him and they went on faster.

Halfway down she stopped and shifted the bundle on her back and felt in the small one for the loaf, to make sure it was there. It was. She tied the cloth back round it tighter and we went down the rest of the hill.
