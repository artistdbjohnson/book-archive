# Chapter Five

The thirteenth day was a Sabbath, so the gate stayed shut, and I didn't have anywhere I had to be. I woke up before it was light anyway, the same as every other morning, and lay on the cot listening. The dye works was shut too. On a working day I could hear the men out in the yard by first light, banging the lids off the vats and building up the fires under them, but on the Sabbath nobody lit anything, and the whole yard down there just sat with its covers on. Even the smell was different. It was still sour, but it was cold sour, without the smoke in it.

Everybody in the city had lit a lamp before sundown the night before, for the Sabbath. My father lit ours every week of my life, right before the sun went down. I hadn't lit mine. It was on the shelf where it always was, full of oil, with the flint beside it.

I spent most of that day sitting at the top of the outside stair with my back against the door. I thought about the woodworker's wife sitting in the sawdust with her face in her apron. I thought about the man sitting on the step down the street from the lane, with his knees up, turning his head every so often to look down it. I wondered if he was still sitting there, all through the Sabbath, or if somebody else was sitting there for him. I didn't go up to the Temple. I didn't go anywhere. When the sun went down and the first three stars came out over the vats, the Sabbath was over, and I went inside and lay down.

On the fourteenth day I opened the gate the same as always.

I pulled the chain around and unlocked the box and set the tablet in its groove, and while I was doing it I caught my wrist on the nail. There was a nail sticking up out of the top of my counter, over near the groove. It had been there since the first month I had the contract.

Somebody had driven it in crooked a long time before me, and it had bent over halfway, so the head of it stood up off the wood about the width of a finger. I caught my wrist on it maybe once a month. Every time I did, I told myself I'd bring a stone in from the street and pound it flat, and I never did. That morning it scraped a little white line across the back of my wrist, and I licked my thumb and rubbed it off and set out my tokens.

The morning was ordinary. A man came in with a load of flax, and a woman with two baskets of greens, and then nobody for most of an hour, and then a string of loads all at once the way it goes sometimes. The scarred guard was at his post. The onion skins he used to stand on had blown off down the road sometime over the Sabbath.

The man with the ink on his finger came a little before the fourth hour.

He didn't come to the counter this time. He came out from the city with another man, a big one in a plain brown cloak with no tablet, and the two of them stood in the shade of the arch on the city side and watched. That was all they did. Every load that came in, they looked at it, and they looked at the man bringing it, and they watched me take the copper and make the mark and hand over the token. The man with the ink didn't write anything down that I could see. He didn't have to. Everybody who came through that gate could see the two of them standing there, and everybody could see that they were watching me.

Around midday the charcoal man came in.

He brought charcoal in from the hills every week on two donkeys, and he'd been paying me for those donkeys for as long as I'd had the gate. Usually he talked the whole time he counted, about his daughters and the price of everything and his back. That day he counted out his coppers and didn't say anything. When I gave him his token, he leaned in a little over the counter.

"Next week I'll come round by the Sheep Gate," he said. "It's farther, but I'll come round that way."

"It's the same toll."

"I know it's the same toll." He tipped his head a little toward the arch, without looking at it. "It's not you. I just don't want to be standing where they're standing." He took his token and went in with his donkeys, and he didn't look at the two men in the shade when he went past them, and they looked at him the whole way.

Not long after that a man came in from the road with his jaw tied up. He had a strip of cloth wrapped under his chin and knotted on top of his head, so the two ends of it stuck straight up out of his hair, and one whole side of his face was swollen out round and shiny. He had a sack of lentils on his shoulder. He paid for it, and then he asked me, talking out of the good side of his mouth, if I knew a man in the city who pulled teeth.

"I don't," I said. "Ask in the lower market. Somebody there will know."

He thanked me and went in through the arch holding his jaw in his hand.

In the middle of the afternoon a man I knew from his copper stopped at my counter. He was a leather worker who came through a couple of times a week with hides on his back, and I'd never known his name. He paid me, and I gave him his token, and then he stood there with his hand flat on the counter next to the oil stain and didn't move it.

"They took another house," he said, low. "Over by the hide sellers. Last night, as soon as the Sabbath was out."

I didn't say anything. I looked at the arch. The two men were still in the shade there, and the big one was looking at us.

"I'm just telling you," the leather worker said. "So it's been said to somebody." He took his hand off my counter and went in.

When I locked up that evening, I stood and looked at the tablet before I put it away. I ran my finger down the marks the way the clerk does. The count was right, but it was smaller. It was smaller than any first day of the week I could remember at that gate, and I knew it was only going to get smaller, because the charcoal man wasn't the only one who was going to come round by the Sheep Gate. The clerk had told me to keep the number right, and the number was right. It just wasn't going to be enough, and I had no way to make it bigger. I couldn't go out on the road and bring people in.

I walked home the long way.

I didn't go anywhere near the lane behind the rope maker's. I went around the lower market on the far side from it, down the hide sellers' street, because that was the only other way I knew to get back to the dye works. That street stinks, so nobody walks it unless they have to. It was getting dark by then, and most of the doors were shut, and there were hides hanging off poles over the doorways so I had to duck under them.

Halfway down, I heard a boy yelling.

It was coming from a passage off to my right, a narrow one between two walls, and when I looked down it I could see it opened out into a little yard at the far end. I went down the passage.

The yard was small, with walls on three sides, and there was a stack of big empty jars standing along one wall, the tall kind they keep oil in. There were two women in the yard, and a boy. One of the women was at a door in the back wall, knocking on it with the flat of her hand, low and fast, and saying something through the crack. The other one had the boy around the middle from behind. He was maybe seven years old, and he was pulling against her with everything he had, trying to get back toward the passage, and he was yelling for his father. "Abba!" Over and over. She had her hand over his mouth, but he kept getting his face out from under it.

Then the door in the back wall opened a crack. The woman who'd been knocking pushed through it, and the other one let go of the boy for just a second to grab the edge of the door, and the boy tore loose and ran straight at me, back toward the passage.

I caught him. I got one arm around his middle and picked him right up off the ground. I've lifted enough sacks at that gate to know what something weighs, and he weighed about what a half sack of barley weighs, maybe a little less. He was kicking. His heels were hitting me in the shins.

Out behind me, back in the hide sellers' street, I could hear men coming. There were a lot of sandals together, faster than men walk home, and somebody calling out, and the light from a torch was starting to come yellow down the walls of the passage.

The door in the back wall had shut again.

So I carried him over to the jars, and I got down on my knees behind them, in between the jars and the wall, and I held him against my chest with my arm around him. I put my mouth right down next to his ear and said, "Shh. Shh." I don't know why that worked. Maybe it didn't work. Maybe he just heard the men too. But he stopped kicking, and he stopped yelling, and he was breathing hard against my arm with his whole body.

The torch went by the end of the passage. Then it stopped and came back.

Through the gap between two jars, I watched a man come partway down the passage with the torch held up over his head. He was a big man, and I'd never seen him before. He held it up and looked into the yard, at the walls, at the back door, at the jars. He was close enough that I could hear him breathing through his nose.

My right hand came off the boy. I don't remember moving it. It was up off the boy and down by my hip, closed, and I knew right where I'd put it on that man if he came around the jars. Up under the last rib, the way I'd done it at the gate on men who tried to walk through without paying. He was big, but that place is the same on a big man as it is on anybody. It would have stopped him long enough.

He didn't come around the jars. Somebody out in the street called him, and he looked one more time at the yard, and then he turned around and took the torch back out of the passage, and the light went off the walls.

My hand came down. It came down and opened, and I put it back on the boy.

We stayed down behind the jars until the sound of the sandals was all the way gone. Then I stood up with him and went over to the door in the back wall and knocked on it low, the way I'd seen the woman do. It opened a crack right away. The woman who'd been holding him looked out. She looked at the boy, and then she looked at me, at my face, and I saw her know it. She'd been through my gate. I don't know when, or with what, but she knew me.

She didn't say anything about it. She took the boy in through the door, and he didn't look back at me, and the door shut. I heard the bar go down on the other side.

I went out of the yard by the passage and back up the hide sellers' street the way the men had come, because I knew they'd gone the other way. I walked home under the hides with my hands at my sides.

When I got up to my room, I didn't light the lamp. I sat on the cot in the dark. My shins hurt where his heels had got me. That was all. I had carried a boy about four steps and knelt down behind some jars with him, and that was the whole of what I'd done. I didn't know whose boy he was, or whose house they had taken, or whether his father was in the upper city by now with his wrists tied. I didn't know what I'd have done if that man had come around the jars. I knew what my hand would have done. I didn't know what would have happened after.

In the morning, out at the gate, I saw the dust.

There was a long smear of it down the front of my tunic, from the boy's feet and from kneeling in that yard. It was that yard's dust, pale and yellowish, and I hadn't seen it in the dark. I stood there at my counter in the early light, with the chain still cool from the night in my hand, and looked down at it.

I didn't brush it off. I unlocked the box, and I set the tablet in its groove.

The two men came back a little before the fourth hour, and they stood in the shade of the arch the same as before. The man with the ink looked at me, and then he looked down at the front of my tunic, and then he looked back up at my face. He didn't say anything. I took the next copper and made the mark.

Now everybody who came through the arch had to walk past those two men to get to me, and the ones who did looked at my face a little longer than they used to.

By midday the smear on my tunic had mostly rubbed off against the counter. By the time I locked up, I could only see it because I knew where to look.
