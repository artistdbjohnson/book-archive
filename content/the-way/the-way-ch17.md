# Chapter Seventeen

The road after that town ran along the bottom of the valley all afternoon, through fields and then through vineyards, with the vines stripped and the leaves going yellow on them. He went slower as the day went on. By the time the sun was down behind the hills he was stopping every few hundred paces, and she had her hand under his arm all the time now, not only when he stopped. I carried the small bundle as well as the skin. She let me.

We slept that night in an old field tower at the edge of a vineyard, a round stone hut with no door, built for a man to sit in at harvest and keep thieves off the grapes. The harvest was done and the vines were stripped, and nobody was keeping anything off them. There were already people in it when we got there, a man and his wife and their daughter, a girl of about ten, and the man got up and moved his bundles over without being asked.

Nobody said much. The girl looked at the woodworker's face. Her mother told her to stop it.

I slept against the wall inside the door, with the skin under my head and my sandals on. The floor was beaten earth with stones coming up through it, and the cold came up out of it through my cloak into my hip, and I kept turning over. Some time in the night I woke up because there were voices outside, low, and feet on the stones, and I lay there and listened. Then somebody put his head in at the door, black against the stars, and whispered, "Anybody in here from the city?"

The girl's father sat up.

"Who's asking?"

"Nobody," the voice said. "Somebody from the lamp shop."

I knew the voice before I could see the face. When he came in and crouched down in the dark, and the girl's father blew on the coals in the corner to make them show a little red, I saw it was the door boy. He looked older now. There were two others outside, a man and a girl, and they came in too and crouched down by the door, and the tower was full.

He saw me. He looked at me a little longer than at the others.

"You're the toll man," he said.

"I was."

He looked at the woodworker then, and his face changed. He reached over and took the woodworker's hand in both of his and held it, and didn't say anything for a breath or two. Then he let go.

"What's happened?" the woodworker's wife said. "You're walking at night."

"Everybody's walking at night now," he said.

He told it low, crouched there with his arms round his knees, and the others listened, and I listened.

He said he'd come out of the city three days before, and they'd laid up through the Sabbath and come on again when it was out. He said Saul had been to the high priest. He said Saul had asked him for letters, and the high priest had given them to him, letters to the synagogues in Damascus, saying that if Saul found any of Stephen's people there, men or women, he could bring them back to Jerusalem bound. He said everybody in the lower market was talking about it. He said Saul was getting a party together to go.

"Damascus," the girl's father said. "That's a long way."

"That's what I thought," the door boy said. "Then I thought, he's not going to go all that way and not look on the road."

Nobody said anything to that.

"And people are saying the letters aren't only for Damascus," he said. "People are saying he's got something for every town on the way. That he's sending word ahead. To the synagogues and the gates."

"Is that true?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's what people are saying."

I don't know if it was true either. Nobody I met ever saw one of those other letters. But on that road, that week, it didn't matter whether it was true. Everybody had heard it.

The door boy said that was why they'd been walking at night. They'd come up out of the city in the dark and gone round the first town by the fields, and they'd slept the day in a dry streambed under some rocks, and come on again at dusk. He said there were others doing it. He said there were little lots of people all along the road, going north at night and lying up in the day, and he'd passed some and been passed by some, and nobody asked anybody's name.

"There's a town tomorrow," he said. "A big one, with a wall. The road goes right through it. We were going to go round it tonight, but it's too far. So it'll be tomorrow night, in the fields."

"He can't walk the fields at night," the woodworker's wife said. "Not with his side. Not over walls."

"Then you'll have to go through in the day," the door boy said. "Through the gate."

He said it the way people said a thing they didn't like.

The girl's father said he'd heard the watch at that town was looking at everybody. He'd heard they'd turned a family back the week before. His wife put her hand on the girl's head.

That was when the woodworker said it.

"He kept the north gate," he said. He didn't look at me. "Four years. Ask him."

They all looked at me, in the little red light from the coals.

I didn't want to say anything. I sat there against the wall with my knees up, and they looked at me, and the door boy looked at me longest.

"How does a gate look at people?" he said.

I thought about it. I'd never had to say it out loud.

"A gate doesn't look at people," I said. "It looks at loads. That's what it's for. People go through free. Nobody gets paid for looking at people."

"They're looking now."

"They're looking for what's different," I said. "The watch stands at a gate all day. After the first hour everything that comes through looks the same, and they stop seeing it. They only see what isn't like the rest."

"Like what?"

"Like a family with all its bedding on its backs," I said. "Or people who come through at first light, when the arch is empty, and there's nobody else to look at. Or people who come round the outside of the wall in the dark and get caught in the fields by somebody's watchman, and have to say what they were doing there."

The door boy didn't say anything.

"The watch looks hardest when there's nothing else going on," I said. "At first light, and at the end of the day, and in the middle of the day when it's hot and the road's empty. The time to go through is the third hour, when the loads are all coming in at once and they're backed up out into the road, and the collector's arguing with somebody about the price of oil, and the watch is watching the argument."

"And the bedding?"

"Put it in a basket," I said. "Put something on top of it. Go through with the loads, behind a donkey, and when you come to the counter, pay the toll on it."

"Pay a toll on our own bedding?"

"Pay a toll on a basket of whatever's on top," I said. "Nobody looks at a man who pays. He's just a man with a load. Nobody asks a man with a load anything except the price. If you go through with nothing, the watch asks you where you're going. If you go through and pay, the collector asks you what you've got, and you tell him, and he tells you what it costs, and that's all."

It was the most I'd said to anybody since I'd left. When I stopped, I didn't know if I'd said too much. The coals had gone down. I couldn't see their faces anymore, only the shapes of them.

"How much?" the girl's father said. "The toll."

"For a basket on a man's back? A copper. Maybe two, at a big town."

He didn't say anything for a little. Then he said, "I've got that."

The door boy said they were still going round, the three of them. They were used to the nights now, he said, and they could go faster that way, and the girl's people were waiting for her somewhere up past that town, and she didn't want to wait. He said it was better that some went one way and some the other. That way, he said, if anybody was looking for a lot of people from the city, they wouldn't find a lot of them all together in one place.

I thought that was right. I didn't say so.

They went out before first light. The door boy went last. He stopped at the door, black against the gray, and looked back in at me.

"The third hour," he said.

"The third hour."

He went.

In the morning the girl's father got a basket off a woman at a farm down the slope, for nothing, because it was old and she had better ones. He put their bedding in it and folded it down small, and the woodworker's wife put the bundles on top of theirs, and on top of all of it the man put a layer of the dry vine cuttings from round the tower, for kindling, the way people sold it by the road. It looked like what it was supposed to look like.

The town was bigger than the last one. It had a proper wall, high, and a gate with a deep arch and big doors, and two watchmen on each side, and a counter just inside the arch with a man behind it and a boy to run his coppers. We came down the road to it a little before the third hour.

We stood off to the side of the road a good way short of the gate, where there was a bank, and sat down on the bank like people resting, and I watched the arch. I watched it the way I'd have watched my own from behind the counter. There were two loads in the road when we got there, and then four, and then a man with a cart of hides who couldn't get through because of a donkey that had lain down in the arch and wouldn't get up. The watch went over to that. The collector stood up behind his counter to see. Then the donkey got up, and the loads started to move again, faster, and more came down the road behind them.

"Not yet," I said, when the girl's father started to get up.

He sat back down. The woodworker sat with his eyes shut and his hand on his side, breathing.

We waited by the side of the road under the wall until there were loads backed up out of the arch into the road, oil and grain and a man with a string of mules carrying timber, and the collector was shouting at the timber man about the length of his beams.

Then we went.

We went in behind a donkey loaded with oil jars, the man with the basket first, and his wife with the girl, and then the woodworker and his wife, walking slow, and me last. I didn't look at the watch. I looked at the donkey's back end. Out of the side of my eye I saw one of the watchmen look at the woodworker, at his face and the way he was walking, and I saw him start to take a step. Then the timber man threw one of his beams down off the mule onto the stones of the arch to show how long it was, and the noise of it went off the walls like a door slamming, and the watchman turned round to see.

The man with the basket stopped at the counter. The collector looked at the basket, and the vine cuttings on top of it.

"Kindling," the man said. "For the market."

"A copper."

The man paid. The collector dropped it in his box and made his mark and gave him a token without looking at him again, and turned back to the timber man, who was still shouting. The watch was watching the timber man. Nobody stopped any of us.

On the far side of the arch there was a street going up into the town, and outside a house on the corner two men were squatting over a pebble game scratched in the dust, arguing about a move, one of them with his hand still on a pebble. Neither of them looked up.

We went up the street past them, and through the town, and out the far gate, which was just as busy, and nobody asked any of us anything.

Out on the road on the other side, the man with the basket stopped and took the vine cuttings off the top and threw them in the ditch, and put the basket on his back with the bedding in it. He looked at me.

He didn't say anything. He nodded once, and then he and his wife and the girl went on up the road faster than we could go, and before long I lost them among all the others.

The woodworker was walking with his hand on his side. He'd been holding his breath all the way through the arch, and now he let it out. His face had gone gray round the mouth. She made him sit down on a stone at the side of the road, and I gave her the skin, and she held it for him while he drank. Then she gave it back to me. It was lighter than I wanted it to be. We hadn't stopped for water in the town. I didn't know if there was a spring before the next one. We sat there until his breathing came back even, and then we got up.

"You watched people come through a lot of times," he said.

"Every day," I said.
