# Chapter Fifteen

The road out of the village went down into a valley and then up the other side of it, a long climb in the gray, with the fields giving way to rocks and scrub as it went. When I got to the top, the sun was up. I stopped there to get my breath and I looked back.

The city was still there. It was a long way off now, back on its hills to the south, and all I could see of it was the wall. It went along the top of the hills in one long gray line with the sun on it, and the Temple stood up above it, white, and that was all. I couldn't see a single house. I couldn't see my gate or tell which part of the wall it was in. From up there it was only a wall.

I looked at it until a man coming up behind me with a loaded mule said, "Are you going on or not?" and I got out of his way.

When I looked back again from the next rise, a little after, there was a hill in the way, and the wall was gone behind it.

The road was busier than I'd thought it would be. There were people going north on it in ones and twos and families, most of them on foot, with whatever they could carry, and a few with a donkey loaded up so high there was hardly any donkey to see. Some of them walked fast and some of them didn't. Nobody talked much. Now and then somebody coming the other way, down toward the city, would look at all of us going up and then look again.

About the third hour somebody was burning the stubble in a field off to the left of the road, down below it in a bottom. There wasn't any wind at all, and the smoke went straight up out of the field in one thin gray line, higher than the hills, and stood there, and didn't bend.

I saw the two of them not long after.

They were sitting at the side of the road in the shade of a big rock, a little way off the road on a flat place. She had the bundles off her back and on the ground beside her. He was sitting with his back to the rock and his legs out in front of him, and he had his eyes shut.

I didn't call out. I didn't hurry. I walked up the road the way I'd been walking, and when I got level with the rock I slowed down, and then I stopped in the road, a few paces off from them, and stood there.

She saw me first. She looked at me for a long breath, and then she put her hand on his knee without taking her eyes off me. He opened his eyes.

He looked at me. He didn't seem surprised. His face had healed up most of the way, and there was only a yellow shadow left under the one eye and down along that side of his jaw, but he was sitting careful, with his hand pressed against his side.

Neither one of them said anything for a little. Then he moved his head toward the shade, the other side of the rock from them.

"There's shade," he said.

I went over and sat down in it. I sat a little apart from them, with my back against the same rock but round the curve of it, so I could see the road and not them, only her foot and the edge of one of the bundles. The rock was cool where the sun hadn't been on it yet. I undid the strap on my bad foot and looked at the heel. It had opened up in the night and stuck to the leather, and it pulled when I took the strap off it. I left the strap loose and sat there with my foot out in the air.

Nobody said anything. I heard her say something low to him, and him say something back, and I didn't try to hear what it was.

After a little she leaned round the rock.

"Is there water in that?"

"It's full," I said.

I held the skin out to her. She took it and drank, not much, and gave it to him, and he drank, longer. Then he held it a moment and looked at it and handed it back to her, and she handed it round the rock to me.

"Our skin split at the seam," she said. "Last night."

"Drink out of that, then," I said. "When you want."

She didn't say thank you. She went back round the rock.

When the sun came round onto them, they got up. It took him two tries. The first time, he got halfway up and stopped, and stayed there bent over with his hand on the rock and his breath going in short, and she stood and waited with her hand under his arm, and didn't say anything, and didn't pull. The second time he came all the way up. She put the big bundle on her back and the small one in her hand, and they went out to the road.

I got up and went out to the road too. I didn't walk with them. I walked behind them, a little way, and I carried the water.

Before long it wasn't a little way. It got shorter on its own, because I couldn't walk as slow as he did without stopping all the time, and in the end I was walking alongside them, on her side. He was on the inside, by the edge, with his hand on his side. She was in the middle.

That was how we went the rest of that day.

At one of the stops, she looked down at my foot, where I'd tied the strap loose and it was rubbing anyway. She didn't say anything. She tore a strip off the end of the cloth the bread was in and held it out, and I folded it over twice and put it in under the strap against the heel. It helped. I told her it helped, and she nodded and looked at the road.

They didn't ask me anything.

I didn't ask them anything either. I didn't ask where his brother's people were, or how far it was. Whatever they said to each other, they said low, on his side, and I let them have it.

What we talked about was the road. She'd say, "There's a bad bit up there," and I'd look and see the stones. I'd say, "There's shade by that wall," and we'd go and stand in it. Once he said, "That's oak," about a tree we went by, and told me what it was good for, how it split, and then he didn't say anything else for an hour. That was all he said to me the whole morning. It was more than I'd expected.

Around midday we stopped again, in the shade of a terrace wall above a field. She got out a strip of dried fish from the small bundle and tore it in three and held one piece out to me without looking at me. I took it. It was hard and salty and made me want water, and we passed the skin round, and it went down by a good part.

"There's a spring," he said. "Further on. Before the next village. There always used to be."

I had nine coppers in my belt and I could have bought us all bread at the next village. But there wasn't any bread for sale on that road that I saw. Everybody on it was carrying what they had, and the villages we went through had their doors shut and their bread inside.

The country changed as we went. The hills got bigger and rounder, with the terraces going up their sides one above the other like steps, and olive trees on most of them, old ones, twisted, with gray leaves that turned silver when the light hit the backs of them. Down in the bottoms there were threshing floors, flat round places of beaten earth with the straw raked off to the sides, and nobody on them now.

The afternoon was hot. There wasn't any shade on the road at all through the middle part of it, and the sun came down on the tops of our heads and up off the stones at us, and my tunic was wet through down the back. He went slower. He stopped more often. When he stopped, she stopped, and I stopped, and we stood in the road in the heat and waited, and nobody said anything about it. People went past us. A family with three little girls all holding hands in a line went by, and the littlest one looked back at the woodworker standing there bent over, and the one in front pulled her on.

A man coming down the road toward the city with an empty pack frame on his back stopped in front of us once and asked was it true what they were saying, that there were soldiers going house to house in there. Nobody answered him. Then she said, "We don't know anything," and he looked at the three of us and went on down.

There was a spring, the way he'd said. It came out of the rocks at the bottom of a cut beside the road into a little stone basin somebody had built for it, and there was green all round it, and a line of people waiting their turn. We waited our turn. There was a boy ahead of us who filled four jars one after the other and took so long about it that a woman behind him started counting out loud. When it came our turn, I filled the skin, and she washed his face for him with her wet hand, and then her own, and then she drank out of her hands, bent over the basin with her eyes shut.

I put my head under the water. It was so cold it hurt.

The sun was low by the time we came up to the next village. It was the sixth day, the thirty-third day after they killed Stephen, and the Sabbath was coming on at sundown.

There were other people on the road stopping there too, for the same reason, and all through the end of the village there were families sitting down by the houses, putting their bundles off their backs, with the light going gold on everything. Some of the people who lived there had their doors open. Some had them shut. There was a smell of bread baking from somewhere, the Sabbath bread, and of smoke, and children were being called in off the street.

A woman came out of one of the open doors and stood in it and looked at the three of us. She looked longest at him, and the way he stood. Then she said there was room on her roof if we didn't mind the roof, and that her husband was away and she'd thank us not to make noise.

We went up her ladder. He went up slow, one rung at a time, with her behind him with her hands ready. I went up last with the bundles.

The roof was flat and swept and still warm from the day, with a low wall round the edge of it. There were some baskets up there and a rolled-up mat in one corner. We put the bundles down in the other corner. He sat down against the low wall with his legs out, and she sat down next to him, and I sat down a little way off, by the baskets.

Down in the village somebody blew the horn for the Sabbath, a long note and then two short, and the sun went down behind the hills to the west. He said the blessing, low, over a bit of bread from the small bundle, and she said it with him. I didn't say it. I sat with my back to the wall and listened to them say it, and when they were done she broke the bread and they ate it. She didn't hold any out to me. There was only the one piece.

When it was dark, she got him lying down. It took her some trying to find a way he could lie, and she folded her cloak up thick and put it under his bad side, and moved it, and moved it again, until he said that was it, that was the way, leave it now. Then she lay down next to him on the bare roof with nothing under her at all.

That was where I was when the Sabbath came in, on a woman's roof in a village I didn't know the name of, a day's walk north of the city.

I lay down on my back on that roof with my sandals on. The low wall round the edge was at my head, and I could feel the heat of the day still in it.

I lay there and listened to her breathing, and his, short on one side, and then I slept.

And the next day was the Sabbath, and we stayed where we were. We didn't walk at all that day.

By the middle of the morning the roof was hot enough that I couldn't put my hand flat on it. There wasn't any shade up there except a strip along the bottom of the low wall on the west side, and the three of us lay in it in a row, and moved along it as it got thinner. None of us went down the ladder. The woman of the house brought a jar of water up at midday and set it at the top of the ladder and went down again without a word, and we drank out of it and saved the skin. I took the strap off my foot and let the heel dry out in the air, and it scabbed over. He lay on his back the whole day with his eyes shut and his hand on his side, and didn't move, except when she gave him water.
