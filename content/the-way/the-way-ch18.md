# Chapter Eighteen

We didn't find water that afternoon. The road went up out of the valley past the second town and along the top of a long dry ridge, with nothing on either side of it but stone and low gray scrub, and the sun on our backs and then on our left side. Twice we saw a village off the road, down in a fold of the hills, and both times it was too far down to go to and come back up from, the way he was walking. We kept on.

I didn't drink. I let them have it. She tried to give it back to me twice and I told her I'd had some, and the second time she looked at me and knew I hadn't, and she didn't try again. My lips went rough, and I kept licking them, and that made it worse. The back of my throat felt like cloth. By evening there was a mouthful in the bottom of the skin, maybe two, and I could hear it move when I walked.

We stopped at dark in a gully below the road, out of sight of it, where a bank of earth had come down and made a flat place. There was no tower and no hut. There was nothing to keep off the cold but the bank. She gave him the last of the water, and he held it in his mouth and swallowed it slowly, a little at a time, and gave her the skin back empty, and she gave it to me.

Nobody ate. There wasn't anything, and nobody said so. She sat against the bank with her knees up and his head on her lap, and pulled the end of her cloak over him, and he lay with his eyes open looking up at the sky. The cold came down off the ridge after the sun went, quick, the way it did up there, and the sweat from the day went cold on my back under the tunic.

I lay down on my side with the skin under my head and my sandals on, and my mouth was dry, and my tongue was thick in it, and I lay there and listened to him breathing. It was a bad night for breathing. Every so often he'd stop, and then catch it again, and she'd say something to him low, and he'd say something back. I don't think any of us slept much. The ground was hard and full of small stones, and I kept finding them with my hip and my shoulder and moving, and finding more.

The stars went round.

When the gray came we got up without saying anything and climbed back up the bank to the road. My legs were stiff all the way up the backs of them. My heel had stuck to the leather again in the night, and when I stood on it, it came away, and I felt it come away and go wet.

That was the thirty-seventh day, the third day of the week.

We walked for maybe an hour in the cool before the sun came up. Nobody talked. I watched the road ahead and the hills on either side of it for anything green, a line of it down a slope, or a patch of reeds, or a tree standing on its own where there shouldn't be one, and there wasn't any. There was stone, and scrub, and the road, and the hills going on gray one behind the other. Then the road came round the shoulder of a hill and dropped, and below us there was a village, a small one, a dozen houses and some pens, and in the middle of it a stone well with a low wall round it and a wooden frame over it, and a woman already at it with a jar.

She saw it too. She didn't say anything. She took his arm.

It was slow getting down to it, because it was steep, and because he had to stop. When we got there the woman with the jar had gone, and there was nobody at the well. There was a leather bucket on a rope tied to the frame, and I let it down. I heard it hit the water a long way down, and I felt it fill and go heavy on the rope, and I hauled it up hand over hand, and it came up over the lip full and slopping.

I gave it to her first. She held it for him, and he drank from the edge of it, and some of it ran down his beard onto the front of his tunic, and he didn't stop until she took it away from him. Then she drank. Then I drank. My stomach was empty, and the water went down into it cold and sat there. I drank until my stomach hurt, and then I let the bucket down again and filled the skin, and pulled the stopper tight, and lifted it. It was heavy. I hung it on my shoulder and felt it pull there, and I didn't mind it.

There was a man at the far side of the well I hadn't noticed when we came down. He was sitting on the wall with his back to us, shaving the back of his neck with a little knife, looking down into a bowl of water on the wall beside him. He didn't turn round. When he was done he wiped the knife on his tunic and threw the water out of the bowl onto the ground and went into one of the houses.

She wet a corner of her head cloth in the bucket and wiped his face with it, and the back of his neck, and his eyes, and he let her. Then she wet it again and held it against his side, over the tunic, where he always held it, and he shut his eyes.

I sat on the ground with my back against the well and took my sandal off. The back of my heel was open again and red round the edge, with grit in it. I poured a little water out of the skin over it and the grit came out, and it stung all the way up the back of my leg, and I sat with the foot out in the air to dry. Then I put the sandal back on.

We sat down on the wall in the shade of the frame. The sun was up over the hill now, and it came down into the village and onto the roofs and the pens, and the village started up round us, a door, somebody shouting at a child, goats being let out. Nobody came out to us. I think they'd seen enough people come down off that road in the last few weeks not to bother.

That was where the man from the offices found us.

He came down the same road we had, from the south, not long after. I saw him on the slope above the village, one man walking on his own, quick, with a stick, and a bag on his shoulder. He didn't walk like somebody who'd been out on the road for days. He walked like somebody who'd slept in a house the night before.

When he got to the bottom he came straight to the well, the way anybody would. He was a thin man about thirty, with a short beard and a narrow face, and his good tunic tied up at the belt for walking, and a cough he kept stopping to give. He put his bag down and went to the bucket, and then he looked up and saw me, and stopped.

I knew him. I didn't know his name. I'd seen him at the offices, at the table nearest the door, the morning they wrote down my name and my father's name, and once more after that, going past my arch on some errand with a tablet under his arm. He'd never spoken to me. He knew me, though. I could see him know me, there at the well with the rope in his hand.

He let the bucket down and filled it and drank, and wiped his mouth. Then he came over and sat down on the wall, not next to me, a little way along it, and looked at the woodworker and his wife, and then away from them. He didn't ask who they were.

"You're Yohanan," he said. "Shimon's son. From the north arch."

"Yes."

"I thought it was you." He coughed. "I'm from the offices."

"I know."

He nodded. He sat with his hands between his knees and looked at the ground. I waited.

"I'm not on any business," he said. "I want you to know that. I'm going up to my mother's village. My sister had a child. I've got four days."

"All right," I said.

He didn't say anything for a little. A woman came out of a house with a jar and saw the four of us on the wall and went back in again. He watched her go.

Then he told me about the clerk and the count. He told me about the morning I didn't come in, and what the heavy man had asked the clerk, and what the clerk had said, and about the runner and the pouch on the stool, and about my room. He told it plainly, the way a man in the offices would tell it, in order, without making anything of it. Some of it he'd seen, and some of it he'd been told, and he said which was which. The dye works part he'd had from one of the guards who went there, and from the dye works man himself, who'd come up to the offices after the Sabbath. I didn't ask him anything. I sat on the wall and listened, and the woodworker's wife sat on the other side of me and listened too, and the woodworker sat with his eyes shut.

When he got to the end of it he stopped.

"It came out right," I said. "The count."

"To the copper," he said. "The clerk said so. He said it three times, to anybody who'd listen."

I hadn't known that I wanted to hear that. I found I did.

He coughed again and spat on the ground, away from us.

"That's not why I came over," he said.

"Why did you come over?"

He looked at me then, for the first time since he'd sat down.

"Before," he said, "you were a count. Every sixth day the clerk took your box and counted it and brought the number up to us, and somebody wrote the number down, and it was right, and that was all you were to anybody up there. A number on the sixth day. Nobody had to say your name. Most of them didn't know it."

"I know."

"Now it's a line," he said. "Your name. Your father's name. Your room, and that the room's empty. Your arch, and the day you left it. And what you looked like." He stopped. "It's written on Saul's board's list now, with the others. One line. I saw it. I didn't write it. One of the scribes did."

I didn't say anything. The woodworker had opened his eyes.

"A count doesn't go anywhere," the man said. "It's on a tablet in a room. A line, somebody reads it out. Somebody says, have we got anything on this one." He coughed. "That's all. I thought you'd want to know it was a line now."

"Who reads it out?"

"I don't know," he said. "Whoever's asking. I only work there."

"Is it only my name?"

"No," he said. "There's a lot of lines. It's a long list now." He looked at the woodworker and his wife again, and away again, quick. "I didn't read them all. I didn't want to."

I sat there with the skin on my shoulder, heavy and cold against my side where it had sweated through the leather.

He stood up and got his bag and put it on his shoulder.

"I didn't see you," he said. "I want you to know that too. If anybody asks me, I came up the road and saw a lot of people, and I didn't know any of them."

"Will anybody ask you?"

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody's ever asked me anything." He coughed. "I'm a man who sits by the door."

He looked at the woodworker one more time. He looked at his face, and the gray round his mouth, and the way he held his side, and I could see him start to say something about it, and not.

"There's a spring a little way up past the next village," he said. "On the left, under the road. My mother's people use it. You'll see the stones."

"Thank you," the wife said.

He nodded to her. Then he picked up his stick, and he went to the bucket and drank once more, and went off up the road north on his own, quick, coughing, the way he'd come down. I watched him up the slope until he was over the top of it.

The woodworker's wife let her breath out.

"Who was that?" the woodworker said.

"A man from the offices," I said. "He sits by the door."

She looked at me. She'd heard all of it.

"What does it mean?" she said. "A line."

I thought about how to say it.

"It's on their list now," I said. "My name."

She didn't say anything to that. She put her hand on the woodworker's knee.

We sat on the wall until he was ready. The skin was full on my shoulder, and the sun was getting high, and the goats had all been let out and were up on the hill behind the village, and the village had gone about its morning and stopped noticing us. My heel was wet inside the sandal again already. I didn't look at it. The woodworker sat with the wet cloth still against his side and his eyes open now, watching the road over the top of the hill where the man had gone.

When he was ready she helped him up off the wall, and I took the small bundle from her.

"North?" she said.

She was asking me.

"North," I said. "There's a spring past the next village. On the left, under the road."
