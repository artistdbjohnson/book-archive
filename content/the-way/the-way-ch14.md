# Chapter Fourteen

I found the well by the smell of it, wet stone, before I found it any other way.

The village was only one street, really, with the houses built close on both sides of it and little lanes going off between them, and all of it dark except for a lamp here and there behind a door. It was hot in there. Out on the road the cold had come down, but in between the houses the heat of the day was still in the walls, and when I put my hand on one going by it was warm as bread. The air didn't move. It smelled of cooking fires gone down to coals and of people living close together.

Partway along there was a bed frame stood up on end against the front of a house, with the cords strung across only half of it and a ball of new cord sitting on the doorstep beside it. I went round it.

The street opened out a little after that into a place where three lanes came together, and the well was in the middle of it, with a low wall of stones round the top and a wooden frame over it. There was a leather bucket on a rope tied off to the frame. Nobody was there.

I untied the rope and let the bucket down. It went a long way down before I heard it hit, and I let it lie there and fill and then I pulled it up hand over hand, and the rope was wet and the water slopped over the side of the bucket onto my feet. I drank straight out of the bucket. I drank until my stomach hurt with it and then I stopped and stood there breathing, and then I drank some more.

I'd never been that thirsty in my life. At the gate there was always the skin on its peg, and a man selling water from a jar at the corner of the market if the skin ran out, and I'd never thought about it once.

A door opened across the open place, and a woman came out with a pot and emptied it into the gutter at the side of the lane. She saw me there by the well. She stood and looked at me a moment with the empty pot in her hands, and then she went back in, and I heard her bar go down.

I filled the skin. It took two buckets. I held it down in the bucket with both hands and watched the bubbles come up out of the neck of it until they stopped, and I put the stopper in and pushed it down hard. Then I let the bucket back down and tied the rope off to the frame the way I'd found it.

Across from the well there was a long wall, the side of some big house or a storehouse, with no door in it on that side, only plain stone going along the whole edge of the open place. There were people lying down along the bottom of it. I hadn't seen them when I came in, because they were down in the dark and not moving. I saw them now, when one of them sat up.

He was an old man. He looked over at me by the well.

"There's room," he said.

That was all he said. He lay back down.

I went over. There were maybe eight or nine of them along the wall, lying on cloaks and mats, with their bundles under their heads or against the wall behind them. Some of them were asleep and some of them weren't. There was a man with two half-grown sons and a pile of pots in a net next to them. There was a woman with a baby asleep on her chest, and a man who might have been her husband or her brother, sitting up with his back to the wall and his knees up. There was the old man. And at the far end there was somebody all wrapped up in a cloak with his face covered, who never moved the whole night.

There was a space between the old man and the man with the sons. I sat down in it with my back to the wall.

The wall was warm. The heat of the day was in it the same as in all the others, and I could feel it through my tunic all down my back, and it felt good, after the stone on the road.

Nobody said anything for a little. Then the man with the sons, without moving his head off his bundle, said, "Where'd you come from?"

"Jerusalem."

"Today?"

"Tonight. At dusk."

"So did we," he said. "Well. This morning. You walked fast."

I didn't say anything to that. He didn't ask me anything else. He didn't ask me why I'd come out, or what I'd done in there, or what I did for a living, and nobody else along that wall asked me either. I sat there and waited for somebody to, and nobody did.

After a little I understood that they weren't going to. I don't know if they'd all come out for the same reason. Some of them, I think, had. The woman with the baby had. I could tell by the way the man with her sat with his back against the wall and his face to the street, and didn't lie down. But nobody said it, and nobody asked it.

The old man turned over toward me.

"Have you eaten?"

"No."

He reached into a bag by his head and got something out and held it over to me in the dark. It was a handful of parched grain, the kind people carried on the road, wrapped in a bit of cloth. I took it. I said thank you. He turned back over.

I ate it a few grains at a time, slow, to make it last. It was hard and dry and tasted of smoke, and I couldn't remember anything tasting better than that. When it was gone, I licked the cloth and folded it up and held it, and in the morning I gave it back to him.

The man with the sons was still awake. I could tell by his breathing. Later he said, low, not really to me, "We'll go as far as we can tomorrow. Then wherever the sun goes down, that's where we'll keep the Sabbath."

"Is there a village?" the woman's man said, from farther down.

"There's always a village."

Nobody said anything after that.

My belt felt wrong. It was light on the left side, where the pouch had hung every day for four years, and the knot of the belt sat different without the strings of it tied in. Twice while I sat there I put my hand down to that side to feel for it, before I remembered. Then I made myself leave my hand in my lap.

I lay down. There wasn't anything to lie on except the ground, which was beaten earth and stones, and I lay on my side with my back to the wall and the skin under my head. The ground was harder than I'd thought it would be. I'd slept on a cot every night since I was a boy. I moved round to find a way to lie on it that didn't hurt, and there wasn't one, and after a little I stopped looking for it.

I hadn't taken my sandals off.

I thought about it. I lay there and thought about untying them and setting them by my head the way I'd always set them by the door, and I didn't do it. I don't know why. Maybe it was only that there wasn't any door to set them by. I left them on. I'd never slept in my sandals before.

The straps were tight across the tops of my feet, where they'd swelled from the walking. I could feel each one of them.

It was a strange way to be hot and cold at once. All down my back the wall was giving out the day's heat, and I was sweating into my tunic where it touched, and all down my front, on the side away from the wall, the night was coming in cool off the street. I turned over once to face the wall to get warm all through, and then it was too hot and I turned back.

There wasn't any sound in that village at all except the people breathing along the wall, and the baby once, and the woman shushing it, and every so often the man with the pots turning over. I could hear the well, a drip somewhere down in it, very far down, and then a long space, and then another drip.

I'd lived my whole life in the city, and I'd never been anywhere at night with that little noise. In the city there was always something. Somebody walking. A door. The watch calling up on the wall. Carts coming in late. And at my gate, all day, every day, the cracked stone in the roadway at the mouth of the arch, and every wheel that came over it knocking on it twice, once going on and once coming off, so that I used to count the loads by it without looking up.

I lay there by the wall and I kept hearing it.

There wasn't any cart. There wasn't any stone. I knew there wasn't. I was a long way up the road in the dark in a village whose name I didn't know, lying on the ground with strangers, and that stone was back at the mouth of my arch with nobody counting anything over it. But every time I started to go to sleep, I'd hear a wheel go over it, the two knocks, and I'd come awake again and lie there listening, and there wasn't anything to listen to.

It happened three or four times. Then it stopped happening.

I don't know when I went to sleep. I didn't think I would at all. The last thing I remember was the heat coming out of the wall into my back, and the straps of my sandals, and the old man next to me saying something in his sleep that I couldn't make out.

I woke up once in the night. I didn't know where I was. I lay there with my eyes open, looking up at more stars than I'd ever seen over the city, and I couldn't think what room I was in, or why the ceiling was gone, and then I could. I knew where I was. I lay there.

Down the wall the man who'd been sitting up with the woman and the baby was still sitting up. I could see him against the sky. He had his knees up and his arms on his knees, and he was looking at the street. He saw me move, I think, because he turned his head toward me. Neither one of us said anything. After a little I put my head back down on the skin.

The next time I woke up, it was gray. The man with the sons was already up and tying the net of pots onto his back, and the boys were stamping their feet and yawning. The old man was sitting up eating something out of his bag. The wrapped-up one at the end of the wall was gone. I never saw his face.

I sat up. The heat was already coming back, even before the sun, the air going thick between the houses with nothing moving in it. Everything hurt. My hip where I'd lain on it, and my shoulder, and my neck from the skin. My feet, in the sandals, were stiff and hot. I'd slept in them the whole night through. I looked at them. There was road dust packed in under every strap, gray, and a red place rubbed raw on the back of one heel where the sandal had worked at it all evening, and I hadn't even felt it until then. I loosened that strap a hole and tied it again.

I got up. I went over to the well and let the bucket down and washed my face and my hands and the back of my neck, and drank, and filled the skin back to the top. The water was cold, colder than it had been in the night. The sun wasn't up yet, only the gray.

When I came back to the wall, I gave the old man his folded cloth. He took it and looked at it and put it back in his bag. He didn't say anything about it.

The man with the pots was already going, with the net on his back and one of the boys carrying their water jar, out of the open place and down the far lane toward the north end of the village, with the two boys behind him. He nodded to me as he went by, and that was his goodbye. The woman with the baby and her man were rolling up their cloak. The baby was awake and looking at everything, and the woman was talking to it low while she worked, telling it they were going for a walk, that was all, a nice long walk. The old man stayed sitting where he was.

"Which way are you going?" I said to him.

"Nowhere," he said. "I live here." He nodded across at a door. "My son's wife won't have me in the house when it's hot. I like it out here anyway."

I didn't know what to say to that. I said goodbye to him, and he said goodbye.

I went out of the open place the way the man with the pots had gone, down the far lane, past the end houses and out onto the road on the other side of the village, where it went on north between the fields in the first gray light. There were people on it already, a few, small, going the same way I was.

I looked for two of them that walked slow, with one of them stopping. I didn't see them yet.

I went on.
