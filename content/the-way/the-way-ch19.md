# Chapter Nineteen

The next village was an hour on, and past it the road ran down a long slope with a bank on the right and a drop on the left, and halfway down, on the left under the road, there were flat stones set into the bank in steps going down, and a spring coming out of the rock at the bottom into a stone trough, and we drank there and I filled the skin again to the top.

After that the road came down off the high ground into a wider valley, with fields on both sides of it, most of them cut, the stubble standing pale and short in long rows. There were more people on the road down there, farm people mostly, going out to their fields and coming back, and some going north like us with bundles, and nobody looked at anybody.

It was hot again by midday. My heel had opened at the well in the morning and stayed open, and every step the back of the sandal rubbed it, and after the first hour I stopped feeling each one and felt only the heat of it, all the time. He was walking worse than he had been in the morning, and she had her arm through his, and every so often he'd stop and stand with his hand flat against his side and his eyes shut, and she'd stand with him, and I'd stand a little way off and wait. When he opened his eyes we'd start again.

In the middle of the afternoon we passed a young man asleep in the ditch beside the road, on his back, with his arms out wide and his mouth open, snoring loud enough to hear from the road. His sandals were set side by side on the bank above his head. A woman going the other way with a basket stopped and looked down at him and laughed, and then she went on.

We didn't stop. We kept on down the valley.

By the time the sun was getting low, he couldn't do it anymore. We were coming into a village, bigger than the one with the well, built along both sides of the road with the houses close together, and he stopped by the first wall and leaned his back against it and slid down it until he was sitting on the ground. His face was gray all over now, not only round the mouth. His lips were pale. She knelt down in front of him.

"I can't," he said. "Not tonight."

"No," she said.

She looked up at me.

I looked up the street. There were people out in it, the way there always were at that time of day, women carrying water, men coming in from the fields with their tools over their shoulders, children everywhere, running between the grown people's legs and getting shouted at. A woman was coming down toward us from the well with a jar on her hip.

The woodworker's wife got up and went to her. I didn't hear what she said. I saw the woman look past her at the woodworker sitting against the wall, and look at me, and put the jar down. They talked. The woman pointed up the street, and said something, and the woodworker's wife nodded, and nodded again. Then the woman picked up her jar and came back down with her, past me, and stopped in front of the woodworker and looked down at him.

"Can you get up?" she said.

He said he could. He couldn't, the first time. The second time we got him up between us, the wife on one side and me on the other, and the woman with the jar walked ahead of us up the street.

It was her husband's house. It was a good way up, on the left, a house with a courtyard in front and a wall round it and a wide gate, and as soon as we came in at the gate I could smell it. Oil. The whole place smelled of olive oil, old oil, strong enough that I could taste it in the back of my throat. There was a press in a shed on one side of the courtyard, a big round stone basin with a stone wheel standing up in it on its edge, and a long beam going back into the wall for the pressing, and the ground all round it was dark and shiny, soaked through with years of oil. There were jars standing in a row along the wall, big ones, stoppered, taller than the boy who came out of the house to see who it was.

The woman put her water jar down and called into the house, and a man came out. He was a big man about fifty, with a round belly and thick arms and a short black beard, and his tunic was dark down the front with oil. He looked at the woodworker hanging between us. He looked at his wife.

"From the road," she said. "He's sick."

"From Jerusalem?" he said.

"Yes," the woodworker's wife said.

He looked at her, and at me, and at the woodworker, and he didn't ask anything else.

"Put him in there," he said, and pointed at a door. "There's a mat." He turned to his wife. "Get him water."

We took the woodworker in. It was a storeroom off the courtyard, low, with more jars in it along the back wall and sacks, and a mat rolled up in the corner, and it smelled of oil like everything else. His wife unrolled the mat and we got him down onto it, and he lay on his back and shut his eyes and breathed. The woman brought a cup of water and his wife held his head up so he could drink it.

I went back out into the courtyard and stood there. I didn't know what to do with myself.

The man was in the press shed, trying to walk one of the big empty jars across the floor from the beam to the wall, rocking it from side to side on its bottom edge. I went over and took the other side of it without asking, and we walked it across between us, and stood it with the others. It was heavier than it looked. He grunted, and nodded at me, and went to get the next one, and I helped him with that too, and the one after it. Then there weren't any more.

The boy was standing by the jars, looking at me. He was about seven. He had a stick in his hand that he'd been hitting the jars with, gently, one after the other, before we came. He was barefoot on one side. The sandal on his other foot had a broken strap, and somebody had tied the two ends of it together in a big lumpy knot over the top of his foot, and while he stood there looking at me he bent down and picked at it with his fingers.

"Leave that," his father said, from the door of the press shed. The boy stopped picking at it. After a little he started again.

The man came over to me. He wiped his hands on the front of his tunic, which didn't do anything, because his tunic was more oil than his hands were.

"You'll eat with us," he said.

"We can pay," I said. I put my hand in my belt and got a copper out and held it out to him.

He looked at the copper. He didn't take it.

"Put that away," he said.

"For the room."

"It's a storeroom," he said. "Put it away."

I put it away.

He looked down at my feet then, at the back of the one sandal, where the blood had come through dark on the leather. He said something to his wife. She went into the press shed and came back with a little clay dish with oil in the bottom of it and gave it to me.

"Put that on it," she said.

I sat down on the edge of the press basin and took the sandal off and did what she said. It stung hard enough that I shut my eyes, and then it didn't. The oil was cool and it went into the open place and stayed there.

We ate in the courtyard when it was nearly dark, on mats spread on the ground near the house door. The woodworker didn't come out. His wife came out and ate with us and took food back in to him, and came out again. There were the man and his wife, and the boy, and an older girl about fourteen who helped her mother bring the food out and then sat down by her and didn't say anything all through it, and the three of us that could come, and that was all.

The woman brought out a big flat dish of oil, green and thick, their own, and a bowl of chickpeas cooked with onion, and a basket with bread in it under a cloth. She put it all down in the middle of the mats and sat down. The man took the cloth off the basket. There were three barley loaves in it, round and flat, still a little warm. He took one up in his two hands.

He said the blessing over it. He said it the way my father had said it, the same words in the same order. I knew every word before he got to it. The woodworker's wife said amen. The girl said it, and the woman, and the boy said it a little after the others, loud. I didn't say it. I kept my mouth shut and looked at the dish of oil.

Then he broke the loaf. He tore it in two, and then tore each half again, and he handed a piece to his wife, and a piece to the boy, and he leaned across the mats and held a piece out to me. It was the biggest of the four.

I took it. Nobody looked at me. The boy was already dipping his in the oil.

I dipped mine. The oil was so thick it ran slowly off the bread, and I got it into my mouth before it could drip, and bit down.

I hadn't eaten in two days. I'd stopped noticing it up on the ridge. Now my mouth filled with water so fast it hurt under my tongue, and I chewed, and the bread was rough barley with the husk still in it, and the oil was green and bitter at the back and soft at the front, and it was the best thing I'd ever put in my mouth in my life. I didn't say so. I ate it slowly. I made myself eat it slowly.

The man tore another loaf and gave me more. I took it.

Then the woman filled a cup from a jar beside her and gave it to her husband, and he drank, and passed it to her, and she drank and passed it to the woodworker's wife, who drank and held it out toward me across the mats. I didn't take it. I shook my head. She held it a moment longer and then gave it to the girl, and the girl gave it back to her mother. Nobody said anything about it. The man was talking to the boy about the jars, and how many times he'd been told about the stick.

The woodworker's wife came out again from the storeroom with the dish she'd taken in. There was a little left in it.

"He ate," she said to the woman. "Some. He's asleep."

The woman nodded and moved the oil closer to her.

I ate chickpeas with the bread. I ate until I couldn't, and then I sat back against the wall of the house with my legs out in front of me, and my stomach was tight and full.

It got dark. The woman lit a lamp and set it on the step by the door, and the light came out across the courtyard as far as the press and the jars and no further.

The man asked where we were going.

"North," the woodworker's wife said. "To his brother's people."

"Far?"

"I don't know," she said. "Further than this."

He nodded. He didn't ask what had happened in Jerusalem. He didn't ask why a man with a side like that was on the road at all. He looked at me once, across the lamp, and then he looked at the boy, who was asleep against his mother with his mouth open and his knotted sandal still on.

"He can stay in there tomorrow," he said. "If he needs to. Nobody's going to need the jars."

"Thank you," she said.

She got up and went in to her husband. The girl helped her mother take the dishes in. The man picked the boy up and carried him into the house over his shoulder, the boy's arms hanging down his back, and came out again, and stood in the door, and looked at me.

"There's another mat in the press shed," he said. "It's warmer in there. The stone holds the heat."

"Thank you."

He went in and pulled the door to behind him.

I went over to the press shed and found the mat, rolled up on a shelf beside the beam, and I unrolled it on the floor beside the basin, where the stone was still warm from the day. Everything smelled of oil. The floor was slick under my sandals, and then I took them off. It was the first time since the night I left the city that I'd taken them off to sleep. I put them by my head.

I lay down on my back. Through the door of the shed I could see the courtyard, and the lamp on the step burning down, and the dark doorway of the storeroom across the way. I could hear him in there. I could hear him breathing, in and out, slow, and her voice once, saying something low, and then nothing but the breathing.

I lay and listened to it for as long as I was awake. He didn't stop once.
