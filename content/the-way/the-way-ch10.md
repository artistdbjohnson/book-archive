# Chapter Ten

On the twenty-sixth day, the guards moved.

I didn't see them do it. When I came up to the gate in the gray before first light, they were already where they were going to be. For as long as the scarred guard had been at my gate, he had stood his watch at the post right beside my counter, close enough that I could have handed him a token without leaning. His tall partner stood on the other side of him. That morning the post was empty. The two of them were standing out at the far end of the arch, on the road side, one on each side of the opening, a good twelve or fifteen steps from me.

I pulled the chain around and unlocked the box and set out my tokens. Nobody said anything to me. When the doors came back and the first loads came up, the guards stopped every one of them out at the mouth of the arch before they ever got to me, and looked at the load, and looked at the man, and asked him where he was going and where he'd come from. Then they let him come on to my counter and pay.

"What's that about?" a man with a load of salt asked me, tipping his head back at them while he counted his coppers.

"I don't know," I said.

"They never used to ask me anything," he said. "I've been coming through here since before you had that stool." He set his coppers down. "Now they want to know my father's village." He took his token and went on in, shaking his head.

I hadn't ever seen them do that before. They used to look at a load when it went past them, the way anybody looks at something going by, and that was all. Now they were asking. And they were doing their asking out where a man coming down the road from the city could see them do it, and nowhere near me.

Around the second hour their officer came down from the fortress by the Temple. He was a short thick man with a red face and a stick, and he walked the length of the arch and looked at where they were standing, and he nodded. He didn't look at my counter at all. He said something to the scarred guard in Greek, and the scarred guard said something back, short, and the officer laughed and went back up into the city.

I understood a little of it. I had picked up enough of their talk in four years to get the gist. The officer had asked him if he liked the view better out there. The scarred guard had said he liked it fine, and it was farther from the board.

I don't think anybody told them to stay away from me. The board had stood at my counter the day before and looked over at the post, and the guards had seen it, and they had moved themselves out of the way of whatever came next. I didn't like it, and I couldn't blame them.

The clerk came for the count at the usual hour. It was the eve of the Sabbath, so people were in a hurry all morning, and the road was busier than it had been all week. He counted the coppers out onto the counter and ran his finger down my marks. The man with the ink on his finger and the big man in the brown cloak were in the shade, and this time there was a third one with them, the man who had carried the old scribe's stool. He had a small board of his own. He came over and stood at the end of my counter while the clerk counted, and he watched every copper go from one side of the counter to the other, and when the clerk wrote the number down on his board, the man with the stool wrote it down on his.

"It's right," the clerk said, to nobody in particular.

The man with the stool wrote that down too.

"It's small," the clerk said, to me this time. "It's the smallest week you've had."

"I know."

He closed his board. He didn't say anything else, and he didn't look at the man with the stool once, the whole time. Then he went back up into the city with his board under his arm, and the man with the stool went back to the shade.

A little after midday a man came up from the city side going out, with a donkey cart loaded with empty oil jars, maybe twenty of them, packed in straw and roped down. They were empty jars going out to sell to the presses up north, so they owed a toll. He knew it, and he had his copper out before he got to me. He was a thin man with a cough, and he made a joke about how twenty empty jars were the lightest load he'd ever paid a toll on. I took it and made the mark and gave him his token, and he went on out toward the road.

The guards stopped him at the mouth of the arch.

They made him untie the ropes. They had him take every one of the jars down off the cart and set it on the ground, and the scarred guard looked down into each one, and then had him tip it over and looked in it again. Then they went through the straw with their hands. The man stood there with his token in his fist and watched them. It went on past the next hour. Twenty jars was a lot of jars to take down and put back. Loads were piling up behind him on the road, coming in, and they couldn't get past the cart, so they couldn't get to me.

I went out from behind my counter. I didn't usually do that.

"He's paid," I said to the scarred guard. "He's got his token. The contract says once he's got the token, he goes."

The scarred guard didn't stop looking in the jar he had in his hands. "Your contract says," he said.

"That's what it says."

"Your contract isn't my orders." He set the jar down on the ground with the others. "A man could get out of this city in an empty jar if it was big enough, and I'm not going to be the one standing here when somebody says that happened at this gate." He looked at me then, once, straight on. "Go back to your counter."

I went back to my counter.

They kept that man there until they'd looked in every jar twice. When they finally let him go, he loaded them all back up himself, by himself, counting them out loud as he went, and roped them down again, and he didn't look at me or at them. The loads that had been piled up behind him came through all at once, a man with a load of pitch and two women with greens and a string of donkeys carrying wool, all of them pushing to get to my counter before the next one. I took their coppers as fast as I could and made the marks and handed out the tokens, and they all looked at me like it was my fault they'd been kept, and maybe it was.

That afternoon the brown-cloak man stood in the shade and watched it all, and the man with the stool wrote on his small board. The man with the ink on his finger stood with them. He didn't come over. He didn't look like a man who was in any hurry about anything.

When the light started to go, I put the leftover tokens in my pouch and the key in after them and locked the box. The whole city was lighting its Sabbath lamps by then. I walked home through the lower market, and it smelled like everybody's bread at once.

On the Sabbath I slept late for the first time in a month. When I woke up, the sun was already on the floor of my room, a long bright square of it under the window, and I lay there on the cot and watched it move across the boards until it got to the leg of the basin stand. The dye works was shut. The yard below was empty. Across the way two old men sat on a doorstep and argued slow over something, all morning, and every so often one of them laughed. I didn't go out, and nobody came to my door.

On the twenty-eighth day, the first day of the week, the guards were still out at the mouth of the arch, the same as before. When I got to my counter, I lifted my hand to the scarred guard out at the end of the arch, which I had never once done in all the time he'd been at my gate. He lifted his chin at me, and then he looked back at the road.

They stopped everybody now, not only loads. A man with nothing but a walking stick would get stopped and asked where he was going and where from, and then let through. People coming in from the road had started to bunch up out there, waiting their turn to be asked. By the time they got to me, a lot of them looked tired of it already, and some of them took it out on me. A man with two baskets of lentils told me they'd kept him out there long enough to ask him if he was from Galilee. "Do I sound like I'm from Galilee?" he said. He didn't. I took his copper.

Early on, a young man going out on foot stopped under the arch, right in the middle where the sound came back off both walls. He had a little clay whistle tucked into his sash. He took it out and blew three notes on it, high, and listened to them come back at him off the stones. Then he laughed and blew the same three notes again, louder, and somebody told him to get moving, and he tucked the whistle away and went on out to the road still grinning about it.

A little before midday a woman came in from the road with a little girl in her arms.

The girl was maybe four. She was too big to be carried like that, and she was hanging over her mother's shoulder with her arms down, limp. Her face was red and her hair was wet and stuck to her forehead, and I could see from my counter that she was breathing fast and short. The woman didn't have anything with her, no load, no basket, so she didn't owe me anything. She was trying to get past the people waiting at the mouth of the arch, and she was saying something to them, and nobody was moving.

The tall guard put his arm out and stopped her.

"Where are you going?"

"There's a woman by the Fish Gate who knows about fevers," she said. "Please. She's been like this since last night. She won't drink."

"Where are you coming from?"

She told him a village I'd never heard of. He started to ask her something else.

"She doesn't owe anything," I said. I said it loud, over all of them. "She's got no load. Let her come through."

The tall guard looked at me. The woman looked at me. I lifted my hand and waved her on, in past the post and past my counter, the way I'd wave a load through once it had its token. The tall guard looked over at the scarred guard. The scarred guard didn't say anything, and he didn't move. After a breath, the tall guard dropped his arm.

The woman came through. She went past my counter half running, with the little girl's head bouncing on her shoulder, and she didn't stop to thank me, and I didn't want her to. She went on up the street and around the corner and was gone.

When I looked over at the shade, the man with the stool was writing. The brown-cloak man was looking right at me. The man with the ink on his finger was looking at me too, and he didn't write anything. He didn't have to. The man with the stool was writing it for him.

The rest of that day nobody said a word to me that they didn't have to. Late in the afternoon the woman came back out through the arch on her way home. The little girl was asleep on her shoulder now, and her face wasn't as red. The guards stopped the woman and asked her where she was going, and she told them, and they let her go. She didn't look over at me, and I didn't look at her long. The guards stopped everybody. The people they stopped came on to me and paid if they owed, and went on through if they didn't, and some of them looked at me hard, and some of them made a point of not looking at me at all.

At the end of the day, with the light going, the man with the stool came out of the shade and stood at the end of my counter again.

"Open the box," he said. "I want to see it."

"The clerk counts the box. It's not his day."

"I'm not counting it. I'm looking at it."

So I took the key out of my pouch and unlocked the box, and the left hinge stuck the way it always did, and I lifted the lid. He looked in. Then he looked at my tablet. He ran his finger down the marks since the clerk's count, the way he'd seen the clerk do it, and then he looked in the box again, and I could see him counting with his lips.

"It's right," I said.

He didn't say anything. He wrote on his board, and he went back to the shade. I shut the lid and locked it, and put the key back in my pouch.

When I came out from behind the counter, the guards were still out at the mouth of the arch, the two of them, one on each side, with the last of the light coming in off the road behind them so I couldn't see their faces.
