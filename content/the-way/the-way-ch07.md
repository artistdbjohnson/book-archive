# Chapter Seven

The clerk came for the week's count on the nineteenth day, the eve of the Sabbath, the same as always. It was a windy morning, which we didn't get much of at that time of year, and the wind was coming straight down the north road and in through the arch, so everybody who came through had their eyes half shut and their cloaks blowing out in front of them. A little before he got there, a man coming in with a load of reed mats lost his head cloth. The wind just lifted it right off him, and it went bowling along the street ahead of him past the date seller's mat, and he went running after it with the mats still on his back, yelling at it to stop. He caught it three doors up. He came back for his token tying it on twice as tight, and he was laughing at himself.

The clerk came right after that, with his board under his arm and his charcoal in his belt. The two men were in the shade of the arch by then. He didn't look at them.

I unlocked the box, and he counted the coppers out on the counter and ran his finger down my marks, and he made his own marks on his board. The number was right. It was smaller than any week I'd ever had at that gate, and he didn't say anything about that either. He wrote it down the same way he wrote all the others.

Then he didn't close the board.

"They want the missing crossings," he said.

"What crossings?"

"Goods went out your gate before first light yesterday." He was looking at his board and not at me, the same way he did the time before. "A bundle of cloth, going out to be sold. There's no mark for it on your tablet. That's what they asked me about, and that's what they want."

I didn't say anything.

"They knew the woman," he said. "That's how this works, I think. Somebody had her written down somewhere. She wove cloth and sold it, and when they went to her house at first light yesterday, she was gone, and the boy was gone, and so was her cloth. They say she couldn't have gotten far enough on foot to go out any other way, so she went out before first light, and your gate is the closest one to where she lived." He tapped the edge of the board with his charcoal. "I'm not asking you. I'm telling you what they said to me."

"There's no mark," I said.

"I know there's no mark. I told them that. I told them your number's right, and it is right. It's been right every week for four years, and it's right today." He closed the board then. "They don't care about the number this time. They want the crossing. They want the toll and the mark, and they want to know who let her through. I don't know what I'm going to tell them after the Sabbath. Probably what you just told me."

"That's all I told you."

"I know," he said. He put the charcoal back in his belt and stood there for a breath, and then he went back up into the city. The man with the ink on his finger watched him go, and then he looked at me, and he didn't come over.

After the clerk was gone, I looked at the tablet in its groove. There wasn't any mark on it for her, and there wasn't any way to put one there now. A mark went on when the copper came in, and there hadn't been any copper.

On my way home that evening, the woodworker's wife was sitting on the step of the shop.

The rag was still stuffed in the gap of the door. The light was going, and up and down the street people were hurrying to get their lamps lit before the Sabbath came in. She was sitting there with her hands in her lap. Her eyes were dry, and she looked older than she had sitting in the sawdust with her face in her apron. I slowed down when I got to her, and I didn't know if I should stop, and she looked up and saw me, and then I couldn't not stop.

"You're the one from the gate," she said.

"I am."

"He said you came. That night, down the lane." She didn't get up. She looked past me down the street, toward the market, and kept her voice low. "There's a new door. The old lamp shop below the potters' steps, the one that's been shut since the lamp maker died. After dark, on the first day of the week." Then she looked back at me. "If you're still walking."

I didn't say anything to that, and she didn't wait for me to. She got up off the step and went inside and pushed the door shut behind her as far as it would go.

The Sabbath was the twentieth day. I spent it in my room with the shutter open. The wind had died down in the night, and the dye works was cold and shut again, and in the morning I could hear families going up toward the Temple, the children running ahead and their mothers calling them back. I lay on the cot and listened to them go by. I thought about the woman with the bundle and the boy holding onto her belt, and how far up the north road two people on foot could get in three days, and I didn't know.

On the twenty-first day, the copper seller came through.

I heard him a long way off, the same as always, his pots and pans rattling on the donkey. He was late. It had been almost three weeks, and I'd started to wonder about him. He pulled up at my counter with his big face red from the road, and he started counting out his coppers, and then he saw the two men in the shade of the arch, and he stopped counting for a second. Then he finished.

"Your gate's got company," he said, and he didn't say it loud.

He took his token and went in, and he didn't look back, and he didn't tell me anything that anybody was saying out on the road.

I locked up that evening when the light started to go, and I walked home through the lower market. I went past the rope maker's lane without looking down it. I went past the woodworker's shop. The step was empty, and the rag was gone out of the door gap. Somebody had fixed the hinge with a strip of leather nailed across it. Then I went on past my own turn, and down the street of the potters, where all the jars were stacked out front with ropes over them for the night, and to the bottom of the potters' steps.

The lamp shop was at the bottom, on the left. I had walked by it a few times over the years and never paid any attention to it. It had a narrow front, and the shutter was down, and there was a dark doorway beside the shutter. Nobody was standing in the street. I stood across from it for a little bit, the same as I'd stood across from the lane. Then I crossed over and knocked on the door, low, with the flat of my hand, the way I'd seen the woman knock in the yard.

The door opened a crack. It was the door boy.

He looked at me the same way he had at the arch, for a breath too long. Then he opened the door the rest of the way and stood back to let me in. He didn't say anything, and neither did I.

It was a smaller room than the storeroom by the lane. Somebody had hung a cloth over the inside of the shutter so no light would get out into the street. There were shelves all around the walls where the lamp maker used to set out his lamps, and they were empty now except for dust and a few broken ones nobody had bothered to throw out, and the whole place smelled like old clay and old oil. There were two lamps lit, both of them sitting on the floor. There were maybe fifteen people. I knew a few of the faces from the storeroom. The man who used to sit on the stool wasn't there. The mother with the little girl wasn't there either. A younger man was standing at the back of the room talking, very low, and everybody was sitting on the floor with their knees up so they would all fit. The floor was gritty with clay dust, and it got on everybody's clothes where they sat.

I stood by the door with my back against the wall, the same as last time.

He didn't talk long. He said the names of some people who had been taken, and some who had gone out of the city, and he didn't say where they had gone. He said the woodworker's name. I knew that was the woodworker's name because his wife was sitting near the front, and when he said it she bent her head down. He said that they would keep meeting, but not twice in the same place, and that nobody should come to this door again after tonight. Then he picked up the loaf.

He said the blessing over it, and everybody said it with him, and I kept my mouth shut the way I had before. He broke it and passed it.

It came around the room hand to hand, everybody tearing off a piece. It got smaller every time. By the time it got to the people near the door, there was only the end of it left, the heel, the hard round end with the crust on three sides. The woodworker's wife had come back toward the door by then. She was sitting on the floor right next to where I was standing, and the heel came to her, and she didn't tear any off. She held it up to me.

She held it about a hand's width from my fingers.

I took it. I was still standing. I took it in my right hand, and it was dry and a little hard, and I put it in my mouth and bit it, and I ate it standing there by the door. It was barley bread, the cheap kind, baked that morning or the day before, and the crust was hard enough that I had to work at it. I could hear myself chewing in that room. Nobody looked at me. She had already turned back around.

Then the cup came.

It came around the same way, hand to hand, and when it got to her she drank from it, and she didn't hold it up to me. She passed it the other way, to the man on her other side. I watched it go along the wall away from me. I didn't reach for it.

They sang a psalm after that, low. When it was over they started going out in twos and threes, not all at once, with the door boy at the door watching the street before he let each lot go. When it was my turn, the door boy touched my arm, and I went out by myself. I walked home the long way, down the hide sellers' street, because it was darker. My mouth still tasted like the crust of that bread when I got to my room.

On the twenty-second day, the man with the ink on his finger came to my counter.

It was hot again, and the wind was gone, and the morning was slow. A woman brought in a basket of eggs, and a man came in with a load of hides for the hide sellers' street, and I took their coppers and made their marks. Then the man with the ink came out of the shade a little after the fourth hour, and the big man in the brown cloak came with him this time and stood a step behind him. The man with the ink put his hand flat on the counter.

"A woman with a bundle of cloth," he said. "Before first light, four days ago. She went out through your gate."

"I collect the tolls."

"Then where's her toll?"

I didn't say anything.

He didn't say anything to that for a breath. He stood there with his hand flat on my counter and the big man behind him, and behind them both a man waiting with two jars of honey on a pole shifted his feet and looked off down the road like he wasn't there. The man with the ink looked at my face, and then he looked down at the pouch on my belt. It was where I kept the box key and the tokens.

He reached across the counter for it.

I took his wrist before his fingers got there. I'd done it at the gate before, on men who reached for the box. I had his wrist in my right hand, and I turned it a little, enough that his fingers opened, and I put his hand back flat against his own chest and held it there against the front of his clean tunic. I didn't push him, and I didn't hit him. I held it there and looked at him.

The big man took a step forward. The man with the ink lifted his other hand a little, and the big man stopped.

I let go.

He took his hand off his chest and looked at his wrist. There were red marks on it where my fingers had been, four of them on the top and one underneath. He rubbed them with his thumb. Then he looked back up at me, and his face didn't change at all.

"That too," he said.

He went back to the shade with the big man behind him, and he stood there the rest of the day the same as before. I took the next copper and made the mark. Every so often I looked up and saw him rubbing his wrist.
