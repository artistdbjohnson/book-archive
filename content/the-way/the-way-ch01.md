# Chapter One

The last copper of the afternoon came from a man with three donkeys carrying barley, and I dropped it in the iron box and scratched his mark in the wax. It was around the ninth hour, and the wall behind me was hot. The wax in my tablet had gotten soft enough that the stylus was pulling up little curls instead of cutting a clean line, so I pressed them down with my thumb. One of the barley sacks had a split in it, and it left a little trail of grain on the street under the arch as the donkeys went on into the city.

I had been collecting the tolls at the northern gate for four years by then, under a contract with the men who held the farming rights, and that day had gone like most of them. A string of goats came through in the morning, and a woman with herbs, and an old man with a donkey loaded with fig baskets, who sold me one fig for later. I set it on the corner of the counter. Up in the seam where my little roof met the wall, a wasp was chewing out crumbs of old mortar and flying off with them, the way it had been doing all week. Across the arch, the older of the two guards was leaning on the wall and rubbing his bad knee.

Then the shouting started back in the city.

It was coming up the market street, and I knew right away it wasn't haggling. Men arguing over a price don't all shout at once, and they don't keep coming closer. This was a crowd taking someone somewhere, and it didn't mean to be stopped. I locked the box and put the key in my pouch without thinking about it, because my hands always did that when I stood up. The stool stayed behind me.

They came around the last corner in one big push and poured through the arch, more men than I could count, older men in good clothes and younger ones out in front doing the shoving. Some of them had a man by the arms and by the back of his tunic, and they were half dragging him and half carrying him. I couldn't see who it was. The press went right past my counter, and then it swept me up with it, and I was out in it with men's shoulders on both sides of me and the dust coming up off the street and sticking to the sweat on my neck.

They took him out past the date seller and off the road onto the open ground outside the wall. It was where people dumped broken stone when a house came down in the city, and it was where they took a man when they meant to stone him. Everyone in Jerusalem knew that.

The crowd opened up into a wide ring, and they pushed him out into the middle of it and let go of him. He stumbled and caught himself and turned around.

It was Stephen.

I had known that face since we were small boys. His tunic was torn open at the shoulder, and there was already blood on it from whatever they had done to him back in the city, and his lip was split. His hands were free, and he kept them down at his sides.

Men were bending down all around the ring and picking up rocks. They were choosing them, picking one up and weighing it in their hand and dropping it for another one that fit better. The first one that came was thrown hard from close in, and it hit Stephen in the face, right on the cheekbone under his eye. His head snapped to the side. The second one hit the same side of his face, up by the temple, and the skin opened there and the blood came down over his ear. He staggered, but he stayed on his feet.

I went forward. I put my shoulder into a gap between two men, and the gap closed up on me, and then a thick arm came across my chest and stopped me.

It was a big man's arm, heavy and slick with sweat, and he smelled like onions and lamp oil. He didn't even turn his head to look at me. He set that arm against my chest and leaned on it.

My body knew what to do about that. My hands had known how to move a man since I was young, and four years at that gate had kept them in practice. There's a soft place under the last rib, and when the heel of a hand goes in there hard, a man folds over and can't get his breath, and then one more step puts him on the ground without anyone pulling a knife. My right hand had already started toward it.

Then a man in front of me turned around and looked right at me.

I knew him. I didn't know his name, but I knew his face, the way I knew a lot of faces from the courts and the market and my own gate, and he knew mine. He had paid me tolls. He knew what I did and whose contract I did it under. There was a warning in the way he looked at me, plain enough that he didn't need to say it out loud, and he held it on me until I understood it.

My hand came back down to my side. I let the arm hold me, and I let the crowd hold me.

Stephen lifted his face up toward the sky over the wall. The blood from his temple was running into his eye, and he blinked it out and kept looking up. He was looking at something, and his face had opened up the way it used to on his father's roof when he was about to tell me something I wasn't going to believe.

"I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God."

I found out later that he had already said those same words to the council, and that was why they had dragged him out here.

The men closest to him clapped both hands over their ears. They started roaring, all of them together, a sound with no words in it, so they wouldn't have to hear him say any more. Then they all rushed him at once. They grabbed him by the arms and by his torn tunic and threw him down, and he landed on his back on the rocks. I heard his head hit.

Two men went over to the edge of the rubble and picked up a block between them. It had been part of someone's wall once, square on the sides, and it was bigger than a man could lift alone. They carried it over to him, stood over him, and dropped it on his chest.

All the air came out of him in one hard sound. It was the only sound he made until he could get his breath back.

The big man's arm took my weight when I lunged against it, and he set his feet and shoved me back a step, and the man who had warned me turned and looked at the pouch on my belt.

Off to the side of the ring, the men who were throwing had been taking off their outer cloaks so their arms would be free, and they had laid them in a pile at the feet of a young man. He was standing by those coats with his arms folded. He was a few years younger than me, in a clean cloak, and his face didn't change. He watched the whole thing the way a man watches grain being measured out, to make sure nobody cheats the scale. He never bent down for a rock, not once. His name was Saul, though I only learned that later, the same way everyone else in the city did.

Someone rolled the wall stone off of Stephen. He turned onto his side, and a rock hit him between the shoulder blades, and another one hit the back of his hand when he put it down to push himself up, and I saw his fingers go wrong. He got himself up onto his knees anyway. His mouth was full of dust and blood. One of his eyes had swelled almost shut. With the other one he looked across the ring, and he found me in the crowd.

"Eleazar," he said.

I don't know if anybody else heard it. Then he lifted his face up again.

"Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

He said it plain and not loud, the way a man asks a friend to hold his cloak for him.

They came in closer then, and they were throwing smaller stones now, ones they could pick up off the ground in handfuls and throw fast from a few steps away. They hit his face and his neck and his chest, and some of them missed and cracked against the rocks around him. One landed by my foot and broke in two. I tried to look at the wall instead, and my eyes kept coming back to him every time.

His voice came one more time, and he called it out loud enough for the whole ring to hear. For about one breath a couple of the men near him held their next stone up in their hand and didn't let it go.

"Lord, do not hold this sin against them."

Then they let them go. He went down on his face in the rocks, and he stopped moving.

They didn't stop. They kept throwing after he quit moving, and after he quit making any sound, until his back and his head were covered with rocks and there wasn't any point anymore, and then one at a time they lowered their arms. The only sounds left were the men breathing hard all around the ring, and out on the road, the date seller calling his prices. "Dates from Jericho. A copper a handful, two for the soft ones." He had never left his mat.

The arm came off my chest. The big man walked off into the crowd without ever looking at me, and the man who had warned me was gone too. The ring broke up in twos and threes. The throwers went back to the young man and picked up their cloaks, and he handed some of them over himself, holding them out by the shoulders so they wouldn't drag in the dust. Then he walked back toward the gate with the men from the council, talking with his head bent close to one of them.

I stood there until the last of them had gone back through the arch. Stephen was about ten steps away from me, lying under the rocks. I never did walk those ten steps out to him. I turned around and went back to my counter.

The older guard was standing at his post with his eyes on the road. He had seen Stephen stop at my counter plenty of times. "He was a friend," he said when I went by. He didn't say it like a question, and I didn't answer it.

I stood my stool back up. A little brown sparrow was hopping around in the barley that had spilled out of the split sack, picking out one seed at a time, and up in the seam over my head the wasp was still working. The last mark in the wax was the barley man's.

A few more loads came through before dark, a woman with a goat on a rope and two brothers with a cart of cut reeds. The cart wheel knocked twice on the cracked paving stone under the arch, once going on and once coming off, the way every wheel did. I took their coppers and scratched their marks the same as any other day. My hand did the work fine, and I watched it do it.

When the light was almost gone, three men and a couple of women went out past the date seller toward the open ground. One of the men had a folded cloth over his arm. One of the women was already crying before they got through the arch, and the older guard turned his face to the wall. I didn't follow them out.

I locked the box and put the key in my pouch. The fig was warm all the way through from sitting on the counter all afternoon, and the skin on it was still whole. I held it a little while and put it in the pouch next to the key.

I walked home past the lower market to the dye works, where I rented a room. It was a walled yard full of stone vats, and it always smelled sour from the wool soaking in them. One old dyer was still sitting by the yard gate rinsing his arms in a bucket, and the water running off his elbows was blue.

My room was up an outside stair over the drying racks. There was a cot in it, a shelf with a clay lamp on it, and a basin on a stand under the one small window. The water I had poured in the basin that morning had been sitting all day with the shutter open, and a thin skin of dust had formed on top of it. I didn't wash. I set the fig on the windowsill and sat down on the cot.

I didn't light the lamp, even though it was full of oil and the flint was sitting right next to it. I watched the window go from gray to dark.

When we were boys, Stephen and I used to climb up the ladder onto his father's roof in the evenings and argue until my mother came out into the lane and called up, "Eleazar," and then I had to climb down. He would say the light coming up over the Mount of Olives in the morning was enough for a man to live on, if he would only look at it. I would tell him the man who owned the house didn't take light for rent. He laughed every time, and we would split a loaf and start the whole argument over the next night.

My father used to say the evening prayer facing the wall, with his hands open in front of him. I knew every word of it. I sat on the cot with my hands between my knees and didn't say any of them.

I looked at my hands in what light was left. There wasn't any blood on them, and there wasn't any dust from that ground either.

Somewhere in the city that night, I figured, Stephen's people were together. I had heard him talk about them plenty, the ones who broke bread in each other's houses and the widows he carried food to, and I knew a couple of the streets where they met. At one point I got up and went to the door and put my hand on the bar. I stood there with my hand on it until the yard gate down below had been pulled shut for the night.

I didn't lift the bar. I went back and lay down on the cot, and I could see the shape of the fig on the sill against the window, where it stayed all night.
