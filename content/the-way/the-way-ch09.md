# Chapter Nine

The extra day the offices gave me was the twenty-fourth, and I spent it at my counter taking tolls.

There wasn't anything else to do with it. They wanted the toll and the mark for a bundle of cloth that had gone out before first light, and they wanted to know who let her through. I didn't have the toll, because nobody had paid it. I couldn't make the mark, because a mark is a copper, and there hadn't been one. And I wasn't going to tell them who let her through. So I opened the gate when the doors came back, and I set out my tokens, and I took the coppers that came, the same as I would have on any other day.

It was a slow morning. A man brought in onions in two big nets, and an old woman brought in a basket of goat cheese wrapped in leaves, and she argued with me about the toll on it, which she did every time, and paid it, which she also did every time. "Cheese isn't goods," she said. "Cheese is food." "You're selling it," I said. "I'm feeding people," she said, "and they pay me for it," and she put her copper down hard and took her token. The man with the ink on his finger came at the fourth hour with the big man in the brown cloak and stood in the shade. He had his sleeve down. When he lifted his hand to wave a fly off his face, I could see the marks on his wrist had gone from purple to a greenish yellow at the edges, the way a bruise goes when it's starting to leave.

A little before midday, a tinker came in from the north road with a big brass basin upside down on his head and the rest of his pots hanging off a pole on his shoulder. The sun was high, and when he came out from under the arch into the street the basin caught it, and a bright spot of light went sliding across the wall and across the scarred guard's face and right into my eyes. Everybody squinted. The tinker didn't know he was doing it. He stopped at my counter and turned his head to talk to me, and the spot of light swung off me and went across the date seller's mat, and the date seller put his hand up over his eyes and yelled at him. The tinker said he was sorry and turned his head back the other way, and it went across the guards again. I took his copper, and he went on up the street with that light going everywhere ahead of him.

The clerk came out to my counter in the middle of the afternoon. It wasn't his day. He came without his board.

He stood at the end of the counter where the guards couldn't hear him well, and he looked down at the wood.

"You could put a copper in the box yourself," he said, low. "Out of your own. And make the mark for it on that morning's line, where it ought to be. Then the number's still right, and there's a toll and there's a mark, and that's two of the three things they asked for."

"A mark is a copper paid by the man with the load," I said. "That's what it says. If I put one there, it says she paid."

"I know what it says."

"She didn't pay."

He breathed out through his nose and looked at the street. "Then I can't help you," he said. He didn't sound angry about it. He sounded tired, and he looked older than he had at the count, with gray coming in at the sides of his beard that I hadn't noticed before. "I've done what I can do. After tonight it isn't the offices, and I don't have a table at Saul's board." He looked at me once, and then he went back up into the city.

I stood there after he was gone with my hand on the box. He had come down without his board, so there wouldn't be any mark of it anywhere that he'd come at all. I thought about that for the rest of the afternoon.

I locked up when the light went, and I walked home through the lower market. At the woodworker's shop the door was pulled almost shut, and there was a lamp lit inside, and I could hear a scraping sound, slow, like somebody working at something with the edge of a chisel. I didn't stop.

I didn't go down the street of the potters. From my own turn I could see the top of the potters' steps, and there wasn't anybody standing there anymore.

At home I lay down on the cot with my clothes on. Nobody came for me that night. Every time somebody went by in the street below, I listened until the footsteps were gone, and then I listened to the dye works, and then somebody else went by.

They came on the twenty-fifth day, a little after the third hour.

The morning before that was ordinary. A woman came in with a basket of lentils on her head and a baby tied on her back, and the baby looked at me the whole time she was paying and never blinked. A man brought in a load of tanned hides, stiff as boards. Two young men brought in a little jar of oil each, and they tried to tell me it was for their mother and not for selling, and I asked them why they had their mother's oil in jars with a merchant's seal on them, and they laughed and paid.

I saw them coming down the street from the city, and I knew who they were before they got to me, because the people in the street were stepping out of their way. There were three of them, with four men from the Temple guard behind them. I had never seen any of the three before. The one in front was an older man, thin and stooped, with a long gray beard, carrying a big wax board in a wooden frame with handles on it, so big he had to hold it out in front of him with both hands. Behind him there was a younger man with a stool and a case of styluses, and next to him a heavy man in a good striped cloak, with a ring on almost every finger, who walked with his hands behind his back.

The man with the ink on his finger came out of the shade to meet them. He didn't say anything to me. He said something to the heavy man, and the heavy man nodded, and the man with the ink went back to the shade and stood there with his sleeve pulled down. He didn't come any closer the whole time they were there. He kept his right hand tucked into the front of his belt with the sleeve pulled down over it.

The younger man set the stool down at the end of my counter, and the old man sat down on it and laid his big board across his knees. He opened it. One side had writing on it already, small and close, and the other side was clean.

"You're the collector at this gate," the heavy man said.

"I collect the tolls."

"Your name."

I told him. The old man wrote it at the top of the clean side, and he wrote my father's name after it, without my telling him.

"How many people went out through this gate on foot this month?" the heavy man said.

"I don't count people. I count loads."

"Then count us the loads."

So I did. I took my tablet out of the groove and turned it so the old man could see it, and I read it to him from the top of the week. I read every load. Eggs, a basket, a copper. Hides, a load, two coppers. I read the salt fish and the flax and the roof beams, and the onions and the cheese and the tinker's brass, and that morning's lentils and hides and oil, and I read the coppers that went with every one of them. It took most of an hour. The heavy man stood there with his hands behind his back and let me do it, and the old man wrote, and once he had to stop and rub his writing hand.

When I was done, the heavy man said, "That's goods."

"That's what comes through my gate that I can count."

"How many families went out through this gate with their bedding?"

"Bedding doesn't owe a toll."

"How many?"

"I didn't count them."

The old man wrote something.

"Who goes out through the little door at night?"

"I'm not here at night. The night watch opens it, for people they know. Never for goods."

"Who did they know, this month?"

"Ask them."

The old man wrote something.

The heavy man turned and looked over at the post, where the scarred guard was standing his watch with his tall partner beside him. He looked at the scarred guard, and the scarred guard looked back at him and didn't change his face. The heavy man didn't go over there. He turned back to me.

"A woman went out through this gate before first light with a bundle of cloth to sell," he said. "There's no mark for her. You were at your counter. Who let her through?"

I didn't say anything.

"Who let her through?"

I looked at him. I didn't look at the post, and I didn't look at the shade where the man with the ink was standing, and I didn't look down at the board.

The heavy man waited. When I didn't say anything, he tipped his head at the old man, just a little, and the old man wrote something.

That was how it went. I answered what I could answer with the count, and I didn't answer the rest. Every time I didn't answer, the old man wrote anyway. He didn't leave a single place empty on that board. Wherever I had said nothing, he put something. I could see the side of it from where I stood, and I could see the lines filling up, and I couldn't read them upside down and that close together, and I don't know to this day what he wrote in those places.

A load came up behind them while they were there, a man with two donkeys loaded with straw, and he stopped back in the street and waited with his eyes down until they were done. Out on the road behind him, a few more loads stopped and waited too. The date seller had quit calling. I hadn't noticed when.

"You knew the one they stoned outside the wall," the heavy man said.

"I knew him since we were boys."

"You were out there."

"I was out there."

The old man wrote that down.

"An officer of this board says you put your hand on him at this counter."

"He reached for my pouch."

"What's in the pouch?"

"The key to the box, and the tokens."

The old man wrote that down too.

At the end, the heavy man asked me where I slept. I told him. He asked me if I had ever been in a room near the lower market where Stephen's people broke bread.

"I've been in a lot of rooms near the lower market," I said. "I walk through it every night."

The old man wrote that down, all of it. I watched his stylus go, and it took him longer than it had taken me to say it.

Then the heavy man nodded, and the old man closed the board. The younger man picked up the stool. They went back up the street the way they had come, with the four guards behind them and people stepping out of their way again, and the man with the ink came out of the shade and went with them. He walked at the back. He was holding his right wrist in his left hand.

The man with the firewood came up to my counter. He set his copper down in front of me and didn't look at me.

"Are you in trouble?" he said.

"I collect the tolls," I said.

He didn't ask again. I gave him his token and made his mark, the first one after that board, and he went on in under the arch with his donkeys. The loads behind him started moving again. A little after that, the date seller started calling his dates from Jericho again, a copper a handful, two for the soft ones, louder than usual, like he was making up for the time he'd lost. The scarred guard watched him go, and then he looked at me, and then he looked back at the road.

The big man in the brown cloak was still standing in the shade by himself. He stayed there the rest of the day.

The afternoon went slow. The loads came, and I took them. The woman with the lentils came back out through the gate with her empty basket and the baby asleep now, and she went past my counter on the far side, by the post, without looking over. A man who had paid me every week for three years with a joke about my father's nose paid me without any joke at all and went in.

When the light started to go, I took the tablet out of its groove and looked at it. Every load was there, and every copper. I put the leftover tokens in my pouch and the key in after them, and I locked the box. The big man in the brown cloak watched me do all of it from the shade.

I walked home through the lower market. When I came to the woodworker's shop, the lamp was lit again, and the scraping had stopped, and somebody inside was talking low. I went on by. At the dye works the men were putting the covers back on the vats for the night, and one of them lifted his hand to me, and I lifted mine back.
